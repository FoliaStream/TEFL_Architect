**Silent Way**

The *Silent Way*, founded by the Egyptian educator and researcher Caleb Gattegno, is based on the belief that knowledge cannot be transmitted; however, awareness of knowledge can be taught. He also believed that teachers must let students figure out answers so that they can discover on their own (Young, 2000, pg. 548). In first language **acquisition**, students learn by absorbing the language that is all around them. Gattegno believed that learners of an additional language could do the same. According to Richards and Rodgers (1987), "[Cuisenaire] rods and color-coded pronunciation charts (called Fidel charts) provide physical for students' learning and also create memorable images to facilitate student recall" (p. 100). It is believed that by using colors to establish a sound system, learners are able to make their own rules based upon colors, which are non-alistract concepts with which abstract concepts such as language can be connected. Richards and Rodgers (2001, p. 100) also refer to this **method** as a problem-solving method because students figure out the sound system based on what they piece together from the input they receive in class. While the Silent Way is not a mainstream **approach** in current **TEFL/TESQL** methodology, there are some educators and students who prefer this approach to language learning with students at lower levels of proficiency.

**TESQL Methods**

While many might not subscribe to an approach that calls for such specific and rigid methods, there are some insights to take away from this method. Many teachers prefer to differentiate sounds and tenses by using different colors to call attention to the differences between grammar and vocabulary structures. The idea of letting people figure out the rules of language is based on an accepted general education principle of constructivism, in which learners construct their own knowledge. Think about how much knowledge you retain when you have to figure out the answer for yourself as opposed to when it is given to you. While getting information right away may be easier for comprehension, people, in general, are more likely to remember information that they had to discover on their own.
