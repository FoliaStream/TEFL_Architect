**Other Important Teaching Methods and Approaches**

There are many teaching methods and approaches that do not fall under the previous categories in this chapter.

✔ Content-Based Instruction (CBI)

*Content-based Instruction* (CBI) is a **method** that can manifest itself in several different ways. According to Davies (2003), "Content-based Instruction (CBI) is a teaching method that emphasizes learning about something rather than learning about language." Instead of focusing on microelements of language such as grammar or pronunciation rules, the focus is on content or contexts in which students would use English. One way in which many teachers use CBI is in a theme-based **approach** to teaching (Davies, 2003). A topic or theme is chosen, the contents of which is new for students, and all language aspects are taught through that topic or theme. For example, the students would learn about the history of the United Nations in the target language, putting the focus on learning the content rather than the language. CBI instruction is more common at the higher levels and is often used within grade schools, as content (e.g., math, science, history, etc.) can be taught in tandem with the second language.

---

✔ Task-Based Learning (TBL)

*Task-based Learning (TBL)* is an approach that requires teachers to think about the real-world task that they want students to complete and work backward from. Rod Ellis, a main founder of TBL, describes the approach as having a focus on an end goal whereby learners complete tasks in the following structure: pre-task, task preparation, task realization, and post-task (Ellis, 2003). For example, an instructor may give students a task of writing a travel brochure or giving an oral presentation. Students would then be sent through a series of structured steps to complete that task.
TBL has also been viewed as a method because it is so open to interpretation, and its implementation has a plethora of possible forms (Littlewood, 2004). TBL is viewed by many teachers as a good recipe because of the structure it gives students. One possible drawback to TBL, however, is that it has a strong focus on fluency over accuracy. This focus means that students may complete a task with language that is fluent but that lacks accuracy (Foster, 1999).

---

✔ Cooperative Learning

*Cooperative Learning*, as the name implies, relies on students learning through cooperation. There are a wide variety of methods that fall under this **approach**, but there is one unifying theme: group work. It is important to note that some students feel comfortable working in groups while others may not. Research on this topic, however, suggests that **EFL/ESL** students may actually master materials better when working in smaller groups rather than independently (Slavin, 1987). Thus, it is important that we incorporate at least some group work in our classes. A variety of methods use the main principles of this approach as a guide.
Teachers in the EFL/ESL classroom can use Cooperative Learning to encourage interaction among students. Cooperative Learning can also make it easier for students of different levels to work together, which allows instruction to reach those above and below expectations. Another benefit of this approach is that it is done by many teachers outside of EFL/ESL, so you can look at lesson plans and observe classes of teachers who do this in other subject areas in order to get ideas on how to implement it in your classroom.

---

✔ Jigsaw Method

A classroom activity involving different groups of students reading or listening to different content. When they join together as a group, they report back and compare what they have learned in order to fully understand each part of the activity.
The *Jigsaw Method* gets students to work together in the same way that each piece of a jigsaw puzzle fits together to form a bigger picture. Each student completes only one part of an activity and then has to work with other students to create a final product. In this way, the *Jigsaw Method* aligns with the **cooperative learning approach**. See Figure 1 for the jigsaw steps.

- [x] Step 1 - Reading assigned materials.
  Students are given the materials that they need to read.

- [x] Step 2 - Expert group discussion.
  After students read the materials, they discuss it and organize information for **presentation** to their jigsaw groups.

- [x] Step 3 - Team reporting.
  Each group reports their information to the class.

- [x] Step 4 - Team recognition
  Games are conducted in teams to evaluate which team has mastered the information. It is also a way to review what was learned.

Mostowitz et al. (1985) give a fuller picture:

"Jigsaw is a cooperative learning **technique** in which students teach part of the regular curriculum to a small group of their peers (Aronson et al., 1978). The classroom teacher divides a lesson into five or six parts and gives one part of the lesson to each student in a jigsaw group of five or six students. Prior to teaching in jigsaw groups, students meet in "expert" groups with their counterparts from other jigsaw groups to help each other prepare to teach their part to their respective jigsaw groups. In the jigsaw group, each student teaches a necessary and unique piece of information to help the group master the assigned work. When the unit is completed, the students are tested and they each receive a grade based upon their own test performance. This contrasts with other cooperative learning techniques in which students receive a grade based upon their group performance. With jigsaw, student cooperation is promoted by structuring student interdependence through the learning **task** rather than through the grading system." (pp. 104-105)

Jigsaw activities have many benefits. For example, they can reduce the **stress** of having to complete tasks in front of an entire class. They also allow students to learn from each other, ensuring they are in their comfort zones and will learn more than just from a general classroom lecture. The teacher functions more as a group monitor and organizer rather than a front-and-center leader. The jigsaw Method in cooperative learning is popular with a lot of **EFL/ESL** teachers. Note, however, that creating these types of student-centered activities takes a lot of planning. Given this, novice teachers might plan to implement one jigsaw lesson per week or every two weeks until they become more comfortable with this teaching method.

✔ Project-Based Learning (PBL)

*Project-based learning* (PBL) consists of using projects – usually involving research – to further learning in the classroom. Project-based Learning has been used in a variety of subject areas and in professional training by many companies. Krajcik, Czerniak, and Berger (1999) define PBL as follows: "PBL is a student-centered **approach** that engages students in exploring important and meaningful questions through a series of investigations and collaborations." PBL can incorporate students' interests, and it lets them work together to investigate assigned topics. Students work independently from the teacher to complete these projects, and they search for information in the language being learned. In this way, students learn a wealth of knowledge about the culture of the people who speak the language they are learning.

Even though students are working on these projects with little direction, there is a great deal of teacher preparation involved. Students also must have detailed instructions in order to keep the project moving toward its culmination. Many teachers use projects as an alternative form of assessment. Projects will many times engage students who may not otherwise be drawn to language learning. Your students will take pride in and ownership of the projects that they complete in your classroom. This pride and ownership will likely transfer to how they feel about their overall language learning experience and encourage them to learn more.

✔ Multisensory Approach

The Multisensory Approach attempts to engage the senses in teaching. According to Rains et al., "Multi-sensory learning, as the name implies, is the process of learning new subject matter through the use of two or more senses. This may include combining visual, auditory, tactile-kinesthetic, and/or even olfactory and taste" (p. 241). Using a combination of senses can increase the likelihood of appealing to different learning preferences.

The most basic way to implement the Multisensory Approach is through combinations of pictures with words, pictures with sounds, or even tastes with words. Additionally, activities that involve drills with flashcards, **realia**, auditory, and tactile involvement also fall under the category of Multisensory Approach and work to improve student comprehension of the target language.

The Multisensory Approach can also speed up the time it takes for students to learn a new language. This, in turn, increases student motivation. Furthermore, the approach avoids using students' native language, a favorable characteristic for many language institutes. This approach is ideal for young-learner classrooms, where students thrive on varied input and repetition and, in turn, are more on-task and better engaged.

✔ Differentiation

As the name implies, *differentiation* is an **approach** in which different instructional techniques are implemented based on the individual needs of learners. Differentiation is an instructional approach that is used across the board by teachers in all disciplines. Wormell (2007) summarizes some benefits of differentiation:

- "While we may show students that they have an inclination towards visual-spatial thinking and suggest that they might want to record the lecture notes as a series of drawings and visual metaphors, we also teach them how to get better at expressing their thinking through the written word. This dexterity – adjusting the curriculum (classroom instruction) and students' skills (helping them learn how to adapt themselves to learning situations that are not in their comfort zones) to make sure students learn successfully – is critical during students' formal schooling." (p. 9)

Differentiation is the art of reaching all students by finding out how each student learns best and then using their strengths to overcome weaker areas. At the same time, differentiation gives students opportunities to adapt to teaching styles that are outside of their strengths, thus helping them to excel in multiple classroom environments. Before you become overwhelmed by this idea, remember that simply writing on the board, using pictures, playing videos and audio clips, providing handouts, and any other assistance are all types of differentiated instruction. Anything you do outside of a traditional lecture (referred to by many teachers as "chalk and talk") to help students learn is a form of differentiation.

This approach is necessary for teaching because there is no playbook, no "one size fits all" guide to reaching all learners. When you begin teaching, try to differentiate at least one lesson per week. The more you teach, the more situations you will come across where differentiation is required. There is a wealth of activities and online readings for this. Remember that you have your colleagues and supervisors as resources for ideas as well. Take it one step at a time, and in the end, you will have a classroom that accommodates all your learners.
