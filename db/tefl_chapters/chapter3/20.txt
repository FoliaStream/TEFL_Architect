**Total Physical Response Storytelling (TPRS)**

*Total Physical Response Storytelling*, which is sometimes called Teaching Proficiency through Reading and Storytelling, has the same premise as **TPR**, which is to give students a lot of language input before they have to speak or write in the language (Varguez, 2009, pg. 2). One of the pitfalls of TPR is that people learn action language out of context, but real speakers always communicate in context. For example, we never just say, "he runs." We want to know why he is running, where he is running to, what he is running to get, and when he will stop running, Varguez (2009) lays out the TPRS process:

- "In the typical TPRS classroom, instructors identify high-frequency vocabulary and grammatical structures and teach them through class conversation, storytelling and reading. Grammar explanations are typically very short and context is narrowed to be the most useful phrases and structures for real communication. A low-anxiety environment is maintained by keeping the target language understandable." (p. 2)

A story is told throughout a class period. Teachers use specific gestures during the story lesson, and the sentences in the story are repeated several times. The first time (or times) through the story, the teacher and students use movement together to represent selected language in the story. Soon, the teacher stops moving and tells the story again, expecting the students to use the same movements in connection to the target language. Eventually, the story order is changed a bit which ensures that student movements are connected to language and have not simply been memorized in order. At the end of the class, students are often able to recite the entire story with the gestures (though they are not usually required to recite or speak at all – their response is totally physical).

Using this pattern, TPRS allows for students to have more in-context language learning and expands upon the idea of giving students a lot of **comprehensible input** before they have to speak or write. TPRS also allows for grammar to be taught on a limited scale, which is not possible in TPR lessons that focus solely on the level of words or phrases. TPRS values the idea of keeping students in a comfortable zone by allowing them to have a lot of input before they have to produce language.
