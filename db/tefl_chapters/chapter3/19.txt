**Total Physical Response (TPR)**

*Total Physical Response* (TPR) connects words, actions, and pictures. TPR is probably the **method** that has gained the most exposure and has lasted the longest. Many researchers and teachers see TPR as an evolutionary step of the **Direct Method**. Since it is difficult to teach actions using the Direct Method, TPR was seen as the next step to address this deficiency. TPR is not new and was introduced in 1925 by Palmer in Tokyo and called "Teaching through Actions" (Richards and Rogers, 1987, p. 87). James Asher is better known for pioneering this method in the 1960s and 1970s. Today, the Internet is full of references to him and his website "TPR World," which sells different TPR products. Asher believed that the best way to make language stick with a person was to make as many connections as possible – called "trace theory" – between learners and the new language (Richards & Rodgers, 1987, p. 87). In addition to connecting words, actions, and pictures, TPR also avoids using the students' native language. Students are able to receive a great deal of input before they have to produce any language, which is very beneficial to young learners who need time to process before producing language in the classroom. If you have ever played Simon Says in a classroom, you have been exposed to a type of TPR. TPR is a nice way to cater to students who have varied **learning styles** because it relies on kinesthetic and visual cues.

Widodo (2005) mentions that Asher's TPR has three main pillars that are based on the principle that says second language learning should be a mirror of first language learning:

1. Before children develop the ability to speak, they develop listening competence. Children create a mental blueprint of the language that will make it possible to produce spoken language later during this period of listening.
2. Children acquire ability in listening comprehension because they need to respond physically to spoken language in the form of parental commands.
3. When a foundation in listening comprehension has been established, speech evolves naturally and effortlessly out of it.

Students quickly learn how to use language and interact with it in a fashion similar to when they learned their first language. Students do not speak much in the initial stages of TPR, as they are usually not required to, but it is a great way to teach commands and vocabulary. Critics counter, though, that students do not learn natural language usage because there is a lack of authentic dialogue seen in everyday scenarios. For example, students do not learn how to form and answer questions in a way that naturally occurs in regular conversations, nor do students learn how to read or write in the target language. Another pitfall is that learners cannot reach abstract concepts through TPR.

*Total Physical Response in Africa*

While TPR is rarely used as the sole way of teaching in classrooms today, variations of it are very popular around the world, and a newer version of it, Total Physical Response Storytelling (**TPRS**), is used in some language classrooms.
