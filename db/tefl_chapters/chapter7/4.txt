**Should We Teach Grammar?**

To begin, it is important to understand that there is some long-standing debate over whether explicit grammar instruction in the **ESL/EFL** classroom is very effective. We know that most people learn their first language via input and trial-and-error communication, not specific and detailed consideration of grammatical rules and structure. Furthermore, according to Weaver (1996), direct grammar instruction and a student’s ability to identify grammar rules rarely translates to grammatical **accuracy** in oral and written output.

On the other hand, extensive meta-analyses of classroom-based language teaching research (Norris & Ortega, 2000, 2001) show us that explicit classroom grammar instruction can be highly effective, with durable and measurable effects on language **acquisition**. The important question then becomes not _whether_ we should teach grammar, but _how_. Consider this quote from Weaver (1996), which summarizes some important research on the effectiveness of direct grammar instruction:

*Focusing on certain aspects of grammar may have some place in the acquisition of an additional language, particularly for adults and adolescents, and particularly after they have acquired the basics of the language. However, studying the grammatical system of a new language does not facilitate the acquisition of that language as readily as being immersed in and attempting to use the language in all its modes (listening, speaking, reading, and writing).*(p. 179)

Essentially, Weaver suggests that teaching grammar separately from other skills (reading, writing, listening, and speaking), is not as effective as incorporating grammar in all areas of skills instruction. In tandem with what we know about the effectiveness of explicit grammar instruction, we can conclude that an ESL/EFL teacher should always consider how they can incorporate grammar instruction into their skill-based lessons, and that grammar instruction should complement, and not replace, our larger communicative **aims**. It should be noted that grammar instruction for young learners is not as explicit as it is for adults, and **presentation** of language patterns (e.g., ‘notice the “er” at the end of ‘smarter’ and ‘taller’) are more often used than **metalanguage** (e.g., ‘notice the **suffix** on comparative adjectives’).
