**Meaningful Input and Explicit Instruction**

All good lesson plans provide meaningful input of the target language, which usually takes the form of a contextualized text (a reading or listening sample). In the PPP lesson format, this most often occurs within the **presentation stage**. According to Herrel & Jordan (2004), teachers need to identify words to be emphasized in a reading or listening passage. Thus, within the meaningful input that we use in the early stages of our plans, we can identify and explicitly teach some target vocabulary, usually 5-10 words or phrases (Zimmerman (2009) recommends no more than 10 lexical items due to constraints on time and learners’ short-term memories). This can be done in numerous ways:

- Photos or **realia** of concrete nouns, including picture dictionaries
- Mime of action verbs
- Photos or **realia** of nouns that have clear attributes for common adjectives
- Simple written or verbal definitions/examples for more abstract language

As a supplement to a meaningful input text, this instruction can occur either before or after the text has been read or listened to. Either order can provide solid repetition of the target words, but doing so beforehand can be a good way to aid in comprehension of the text.

If you decide to select and pre-teach some words from a text before reading/listening to it, you then must decide whether to instruct those words inductively or deductively (a dichotomy that we will see again in relation to grammar). To teach vocabulary deductively would be to focus on the form of the word first, showing and pronouncing a word or list of words and proceeding to establish meaning afterward. To teach vocabulary inductively, on the other hand, is to first establish meaning via one or more of the methods listed above, often by trying to elicit the words from the students before writing or saying them. For example, writing a word like “sailboat” on the board and asking, “Does anybody know what this word, sailboat, means?” is deductive in nature, but holding up an image of a sailboat and asking, “Does anyone know what this is? What do we call it in English?” is inductive in nature. This type of elicitation is important in inductive teaching and can be a useful tool in **EFL** classes in general, as it not only focuses on meaning from the outset, but it also allows the teacher to establish what the students already know about the target texts before any instruction takes place.

Whatever words and **collocations** you introduce to your students, presenting them in context and providing multiple opportunities to interact with the words will enhance retention.
