**Linguistic Understanding - Five Ways of Understanding**

By teaching language, you are an _applied linguist_, which means you are applying the knowledge of the linguistics field in a classroom. Linguistics contains five different ways of understanding language: **phonology**, **morphology**, **syntax**, **semantics**, and **pragmatics**. The authors Freeman and Freeman (2004) argue that in order for students to really 'know a word' and use it appropriately, they must understand how all five of these ways of understanding apply to the target vocabulary items. That means that a good language teacher will take all five ways of understanding into consideration whenever teaching new vocabulary. They are explained below. Click on the name of each type of understanding to learn more.

- *Phonology (Sound)*: Phonological understanding is recognizing the way that a language organizes phonemes (i.e., sounds) in order to convey meaning at the word or sentence level. For example, during listening exercises, **ESL/EFL** students may not be able to identify certain pronunciation features of a word (or connected speech) unless those features are introduced and practiced beforehand. Phonology also includes pronunciation and how we form the sounds physically in our vocal tract. Students will not know how to physically form the pronunciation or sound if that sound does not exist in their native language. Use this interactive tool to help students.

- *Morphology (Word formation)*: Morphological understanding is understanding word structure, root words, new word creating, word changes, and **morphemes** that go into building words. For example (and as we have seen), prefixes and suffixes add to a word to create a more complex word. Imagine a learner knows that _amuse_ can become a noun by adding the **suffix**-_ment_. He might come across the word _surprise_ and deduce that he can add the same suffix to form the word _surprisement_. This learner lacks full morphological understanding of the word _surprise_. We find other examples in verb conjugations, plurals, possessives, and etymology.

- *Syntax (Sentence Formation):* Syntactic understanding is understanding how words function together to create units like phrases, clauses or sentences. This includes word order, sentence formation, question formation, and parts of speech (articles, nouns, verbs, etc.). For example, the verb say takes a direct object as in "Jerry said that he didn't see the car coming" and "Martha finally said farewell." To add indirect objects to these sentences, you need to insert the word to "Jerry said to the officer that he didn't see the car coming" and "Martha finally said farewell to her lava lamp from the 1980s."

- *Semantics (Meaning):* Semantic understanding is knowing the meaning of language, such as a word's common synonyms, definition(s), and metaphorical meanings. Learning any new vocabulary is a form of semantic understanding, but sometimes there are ways to explicitly help students with semantic confusion.

- *Pragmatics (Context):* Pragmatic understanding is knowing what language is appropriate to use according to the context or situation surrounding it, which is often a question of formality. Students often learn pragmatics implicitly, observing what is appropriate to say to a peer, to an elder, in a classroom, etc. For example, pragmatic understanding involves knowing when it is appropriate to use a particular word or phrase for everyday situations. Note that passed *away* means technically the same thing as *dropped dead or kicked the bucket*. However, the latter two are not appropriate in certain social situations. When expressing sympathy to a friend, among these three choices of expression, you would likely say, "I'm sorry your grandmother passed away" and not "I'm sorry your grandma kicked the bucket."
