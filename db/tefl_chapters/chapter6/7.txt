**Collocations and Phrasal Verbs**

When you introduce certain words in your classroom, you may wish to teach associated collocations as well. This will increase the likelihood that your students will use the words properly. *Collocations* refer to the way in which words go together. For example, there are verbs that are frequently followed by certain nouns. *To see more examples, click through the four pages of the book below. Click the right side will turn the page forward. Clicking the left side will turn the page back.*

---

**Verb "commit"**

Take the verb "commit," for example, which is often followed by an act of wrongdoing:

- commit murder
- commit a crime
- commit treason

We would never say, "commit a favor." But this might be a logical conclusion if a student looked at the meaning of "commit" in the previous examples, which at its root means "to do."

**Noun "appointment"**

Consider the following verbs often used with the word "appointment," whose synonym is "meeting":

- make an appointment (You can't say "make a meeting")
- keep an appointment (You _can_ say "keep a meeting")
- break an appointment (You can't say "break a meeting")
- cancel an appointment (You _can_ say "cancel a meeting")

For more information on collocations, check your local bookstore or search online for collocation dictionaries and lists. The following online resource offers more information on this topic:

**Phrasal Verbs**

*Phrasal verbs* are verbs that contain a main verb and a particle, or a verb and an adverb. These are particularly difficult for **ESL/EFL** learners, as one can know the meaning of the verb as well as the meaning of the particle alone as a preposition, but putting the verb and particle together does not usually create a predictable meaning (Celce-Murcia & Larsen-Freeman, 1999, p. 425). Consider the difference between the following phrasal verbs using *pick*:

- Judy **picked out** a new outfit for the party.
- We need to pick up the dry cleaning soon.
- Otis **picks on** his little brother sometimes.

We also see many variations of meaning with the verb *get*:

- We need to **get back** to work.
- Get out the champagne – it's time to celebrate!
- He **got out** of the car before I finished my story.
- My grandpa **gets up** at 6 AM every day.
- Let's **get in** the taxi before there's too much traffic.
- We'll **get on** Interstate 90 to go to the airport.
- **Get over** yourself!
- Carlos needs to **get over** his recent breakup.
