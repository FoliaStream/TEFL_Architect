**Idioms**

There are several types of figurative (non-literal) language in English. Many of these forms of figurative language can be difficult for even our more advanced students because (just as with phrasal verbs) even a student who knows all of the individual words within a phrase or sentence may not be able to predict the overall meaning of that phrase/sentence.

Here are four of the most common types of figurative language:

- **Metaphor**: "Time is a thief in the night."
- **Simile** (uses *like* or *as*): "His words cut like a knife."
- **Proverb** (derived from folk tales): "Slow and steady wins the race."
- **Idioms**: "He's in the dog house," "Let's bury the hatchet," etc.

Of the four categories, idioms are used most frequently in everyday **discourse** (Cooper, 1998). Phrasal verbs, in particular, often have idiomatic meanings, as we have seen.

Cooper (1998) describes two types of idioms: *frozen (or fixed)* idioms and *syntactically flexible idioms*. Examples of frozen idioms include:

- John kicked the bucket. • The bucket was kicked by John.
- We called it a day. • A day was called by us.

In both cases, you cannot change the sentence to the **passive voice** and still understand the idiomatic meaning.

An example of a flexible **idiom** is the following:

- The president rolled out the red carpet for the royal couple. • The red carpet was rolled out for the royal couple.

Frozen idioms are easier to learn, as they do not vary in form and "can be internalized as a single **lexical item**" (Cooper, 1998). Some idioms are easier to visualize than others. We call these idioms more *transparent*. For example, "We need to shake a leg" is more easily visualized in context than, "Don't let the cat out of the bag." Shaking a leg is relatable to the concept of motion. By contrast, cats and bags have little to do with divulging information.

Some expressions can be meant either literally or figuratively, and phrasal verbs can often be used idiomatically, depending on the context. In the following examples, expressions (a) are idiomatic, while expressions (b) are interpreted literally.

- **a)** She has been in the hospital, but I think she's going to make it.
- **b)** What are we having for dinner? Who's going to make it?

- **a)** They refused dessert; they've been trying to cut down lately.
- **b)** Who cut down that old oak tree in the park?

- **a)** The economy has to turn around eventually.
- **b)** We need to turn our chairs around so that the sun isn't in our eyes.

Here are a few ideas for teaching idioms, as suggested by Cooper (1998):

- Have students keep a vocabulary log that includes an "idiom section."
- Have students choose a favorite idiom to report to the class every week and ask them why this idiom appeals to them.
- Have students draw idioms.
- Have students act out idioms, with others guessing the idiom and meaning.
