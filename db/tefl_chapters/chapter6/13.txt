- **Phonological understanding** - There is no explicit phonological focus on the words here, but that has likely been provided previously when the words were originally taught (if the teacher modeled pronunciation and elicited repetition from the students). Furthermore, the students will no doubt say the target adjectives repeatedly in Part 8 and while working in Part C. The teacher can listen closely and provide corrective feedback, thereby providing some phonological focus.
- **Morphological understanding** - There is no explicit focus on the morphology of the target words in this exercise.**
Adjectives can be expanded via suffixes to create comparative and superlative forms of the adjectives (e.g.,
hot/hotter/hottest, pretty/prettier/prettiest, etc.), but that should be the focus for a later lesson that explicitly teaches
and practices that structure and function of language.
- **Syntactic understanding** - There is a major focus on syntactic understanding in Parts B and C, as the students have to create and fill in complete sentences that repeatedly modify nouns with the adjectives, thereby placing the target leads within syntax that is natural and communicative.
- **Semantic understanding** - There is also a major focus on semantic understanding throughout the exercise. The students should have some knowledge of the meaning of the original adjectives, and the first step of the exercise (Part A) requires them to pair those adjectives with their antonyms. Furthermore, in connecting all of the adjectives with nouns that exemplify them, the students should further cement their semantic understanding of each.
- **Pragmatic understanding** - There is no explicit focus on pragmatic understanding in the exercise, but it is entirely possible that one or more of the sentences that the students create in Part 8 (and write on the board in Part C) will use one or more of the adjectives in a pragmatic inappropriate way. For example, if the example sentences used ugly or old in connection with a person, the teacher could facilitate a discussion about insults and the appropriacy of discussing someone's age. Likewise, if a sentence that the students create included "Kevin is slow," the teacher could start with a semantic explanation (**slow as the opposite of fast vs. slow as the opposite of smart/intelligent**). Then, a discussion of **pragmatics** might follow (i.e., that **slow** as the opposite of **smart/intelligent** is rude and inappropriate in a classroom setting).
