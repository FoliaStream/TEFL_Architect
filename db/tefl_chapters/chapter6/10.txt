**Association, Lexical Sets, and Cognates**

Whenever we teach **lexis** in our classrooms, we should always strive to have our students make connections between the target vocabulary and other words and concepts. Three examples of connections that we can make are *association*, *lexical sets*, and *cognates*.

**Association**: *Association* is useful when it comes to learning new vocabulary. The human mind connects ideas and concepts together as they relate to certain experiences. Once a concept is recalled, other related concepts are remembered as well. This is called association, and it can be used in teaching vocabulary. In reading, for example, a teacher pauses after reading out loud to ask students to share what other ideas come to mind. Doing this will help the students remember new vocabulary in the reading. Association by similarity connects words with similar meanings (e.g., "grief" with "sadness" and "sorrow"), or it can include personal connections made by an individual (e.g., "coffee" with "hot" and "drink").

**Lexical Sets**: As in the example above with the word "grief," which is associated with different, more familiar words, other words that are related in meaning are part of a *lexical set*. A lexical set is a group of words that share the same topic (lexis), form (grammar), or even pronunciation pattern. A lexical set under the lexis "pet" includes "cat," "dog," and "hamster." A lexical set under the grammar form "adjective" includes "beautiful," "yellow," and "fragrant." A lexical set under the pronunciation of /oʊ/ includes "go," "show," and "low."

**Cognates**: *Cognates* are useful in learning vocabulary because they are similar to words in the language learners' mother tongues. Cognates in two languages can be similar not only in meaning, but also in spelling and pronunciation. In English and Spanish, "car" and "carro" are cognates. In English and German, "milk" and "milch" are cognates. Knowing cognates helps in language learning. However, not all words that are similar are cognates. The Spanish "embarazada" means pregnant, not "embarrassed," for example. In German, the noun "Arm" means the same in English, but it also means "poor" when used as an adjective. Words that appear to be cognates but are not are called *false friends*.
