**Organizing and Grouping Students**

The student-centered approach is a collaborative one and thus heavily relies on students working in pairs and groups. For some, this may be a very different class dynamic than they are used to; they may feel the teacher isn’t really teaching, or they may feel unfamiliar and uncomfortable with his new approach. To the extent that it is possible, it will be helpful to take time at the onset of a course to elicit opinions about this methodology. Finding out how students feel can help you plan how to incorporate this style of work into your lessons, as well as what types of activities will be most suitable.

When you’ve made the decision to have students work in pairs or groups, the question becomes how to put them together. The following methods can be used to determine the most appropriate groupings.

*Random Groupings*: One common method for random grouping is to make a set of name cards for the class and then shuffle them to put students together. The advantage of this is that every student will get a chance to work with every other student at some point in time. This is the most truly random way to group students. A variation of this technique involves putting pertinent information about each student (e.g., interest, proficiency, and native language) on the back of each card, which allows you to exercise some degree of control over who works together while still appearing to create totally random groupings.

Streaming: This strategy involves putting students together according to their language ability, either mixing proficiency levels or grouping them at the same level. This would seem to make sense at face value, but it can also lead to a loss of a cooperative framework in the class - stronger students may feel unchallenged, while weaker ones feel overwhelmed. For this and other reasons, most teachers prefer arranging students in an ad hoc manner. If you do decide to stream your students, give different tasks to each group, and mix up the groups from time to time.

*Assigned Grouping*: One grouping strategy is to have those who like and get along with each other always work together. The advantage is that there will always be a good rapport between the students. This method is not ideal, however. And can have many pitfalls. Relationships can change, and our observations of who gets along with whom may not be accurate. Additionally, certain students may be guided and in any lead to the creation of student cliques. For this reason and others, teachers generally avoid this method. However, if you have a cohort-type group of students (i.e., they have been together as a class for some time), this arrangement may be feasible.

Another grouping strategy is to have students who happen to be sitting next to each other work together. This may not always work, as some students often like to always sit next to a particular classmate. If your students don’t already know each other, however, this will successfully create random groupings during the first few days of class. Students can also be organized by assigning them a particular letter or number and having those who share the same letter or number work together. You can also have them pick the letter or number. Those who choose letters A-M work with those who chose M-Z or pair off even numbers with odd numbers.

Arranging by task is another method. For example, in a *find someone who…* activity, you may decide to have students who share a common answer work together, or you may arrange them according to preferences discovered through an activity.

Regardless of the method used, you are encouraged to change pairings and groupings during the course of a lesson to make things dynamic and keep the energy level high. Also, keep cultural context in mind when arranging students (e.g., putting students of different genders together may be an issue in some countries).
