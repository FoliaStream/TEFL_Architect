**Creating Scaffolded Activities**

No matter how creative or dynamic an activity is, it will fall flat if not appropriately set up in a way that students can understand and complete. To that end, all activities need scaffolding, which is a concept that we will address first before looking at sample activities.

Scaffolding is a term used to describe the process of breaking learning into manageable chunks and providing a structure by which students can follow and digest that information (Haynes, 2015). Prior to running any activity in class, you need to think about how much scaffolding is needed for students to successfully complete your activity. The amount of support and direction that is needed will depend on a variety of factors, such as the proficiency level, age of the students, and the complexity of the activity. Figure 3 provides some general guidelines to help you create activities that are appropriately scaffolded for your students.

Figure 3. Creating scaffolded activities

*Step 1* 
- Tap into students’ existing English skills and connect new language to language students already have and can use. Then, pre-teach any new language that is key to task completion.
- Use level-appropriate language to provide students with simple steps to complete the task. Use written text or visuals as support.
- Model the activity for the students. If needed, show the process and desired end product.


*Step 2* 
- Have students work as a class, independently, or in pairs/groups to complete the task (e.g., role play, writing, reading).
- Circulate for assistance, providing support where needed.

*Step 3* 
- Review language learned in activity, encouraging as much student-centered involvement as possible through pair sharing or through volunteer students reviewing the activity (i.e., do not just read answers to students).

*Step 4*
- Transition to next activity


Regardless of whether your activity is a simple worksheet or a complex role play, the steps in figure 3 show that there is a lot to consider before students actually start an activity. If you are giving them a worksheet, for example, you may want to complete the first two questions together to be sure students understand what needs to be done before having them complete the rest individually or in groups. If you are giving students something to read or listen to, you’ll want to prepare them for the topic first, perhaps through the use of visuals, simple discussion questions, and/or introduction to key vocabulary to get them mentally prepared to hear or read the new content.

For many tasks, it can also be helpful to model what you want students to do and write very simple steps on the board (possibly including time limits as well). This type of visual support is especially useful with lower-level students, who may have a difficult time following spoken directions or for more visual learners in general.

Keep these scaffolding ideas in mind as you read about more sample activities in the next sections. Finally, don’t fret if you start an activity and find that some or all students look confused; it is perfectly fine to re-start and add more scaffolding.
