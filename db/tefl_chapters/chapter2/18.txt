**Individual Differences**

Past Research

There have been many attempts to classify individual differences in an effort to help teachers connect with students and enhance learning. In recent times, Neil Fleming’s VARK Learning Style model was thought by many to be a key to understanding how to accommodate learner differences in the classroom. The VARK Learning Styles Model is summarized in Figure 7.

---

*Figure 7.* VARK Learning Styles Model

Visual Learners: Visual learners acquire knowledge through visual depictions of information such as graphs, flow charts, and symbolic arrows, but they may have a hard time concentrating while listening to a subject being explained to them. They benefit from illustrations, colorful pictures, posters, multimedia, and presentations that use different color highlighters. Visual learners tend to memorize information in pictures.

Aural (auditory) Learners: Aural learners benefit from tutorials, lectures, audiobooks, and discussing topics with other students; they tend to remember interesting examples and identify visually at where they can hear your blog, close to the CD player(s), but they do not necessarily need to pay attention to what is happening at the front of the classroom. Aural learners acquire knowledge by reading aloud, listening to others, and by verbalizing lessons to themselves.

Read-Write Learners: Read-write learners acquire new information directly from the written word (e.g., textbooks). Unlike visual learners, they would only benefit from a graph or flow chart if they could describe the information presented in their own words. Read-write learners benefit from definitions, arranging items into hierarchies with headings, lists, and bullet points, and rewriting information. Many academics have a strong preference for this mode.

Kineshletic Learners: Kineshletic learners do well with direct experience and practice (real or simulated). They remember what was done, but they may have difficulty recalling what was said or seen. They speak with their hands and with gestures, and they are great performers. They tend to sit at the back of the classroom so that they can move around when bored. They need to be active and take frequent breaks. They benefit from field trips, using all senses (eight, touch, taste, smell, and hearing), and other hands-on experience.

Despite VARK’s initial popularity, it quickly came under heavy criticism. Today, we now know that there is little empirical evidence to support such clear distinctions in modes of learning and that it is not possible to label learners as one style (Prashing, 2015). Furthermore, what someone prefers as their mode for learning may not necessarily be what is the most effective for them. In other words, appealing to an individual’s “best” method for learning is much more complex.

That said, reflecting on these learning modes collectively is worthwhile because it reminds us of the importance of varying dynamics and activities in our classes. When teaching vocabulary, for instance, teachers can use a variety of different modes for learning. For example, teachers may use all the following activities to address the same vocabulary words:

- Use pictures to accompany new words
- Have students write new words in sentences
- Listen to the new words used in a dialogue
- Read the new words in a story
- Use the new words in a game or interactive activity

When done appropriately, a student-centered class conducted in a variety of modes is likely to increase student motivation and retention. Another important aspect of individual differences is the consideration of student variation in abilities. Your EFL classes will have a small-to-large range in language abilities, having students both above and below the class norm. Brown and Lee (2015) offer the following advice to accommodate these differences:

- Design activities that have a range of easy-to-difficult portions or questions.
- In a discreet manner, ask easier questions to students below the norm and the more difficult questions to those above.
- To avoid the strong students dominating, make activities that involve everyone.
- Be strategic in grouping by evaluating the task to determine if you should place mixed abilities or similar abilities together.
- Include both small group and pair work to allow you time to circulate and help those below the norm.
