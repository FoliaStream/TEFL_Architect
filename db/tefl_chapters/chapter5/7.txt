**Creating Course Goals**

After delivering a **needs analysis**, which may include diagnostic language assessment, the next step is to formulate some overarching goals for your students' course of study.

These *course goals* differ from the *learning objectives* for an individual lesson. Learning objectives usually state what a student will be able to do at the end of a lesson, but course goals state the **larger aims** of an extended period of time (Brown & Lee, 2015). For that reason, while lesson objectives are usually stated using SWBT language, course goals are often stated in terms of broad course content and tasks, or what a student _will do_ over the length of a course.

Some goals for a high-beginners' course of general English study could include:

- **Throughout this *high-beginners' EFL course, students will...***
  - gain confidence in reading high-beginner and intermediate EFL texts and **articles**
  - write several short texts, such as paragraphs, summaries of readings, and e-mails
  - improve their listening skills by comprehending short videos and recordings
  - **practice** speaking skills and improve pronunciation by participating in conversations and giving several short presentations
  - comprehend and communicate with beginner-to-intermediate grammar structures, including past/present/future simple and continuous, as well as present perfect
  - build vocabulary around a variety of real-world topics
  - develop a general awareness of English language culture

By design, these goals are quite general in nature and are intended to provide students (and their teacher) with a broad scope of what the students will be asked to do over the course of study.

Once the goals of a course of study have been determined, the next steps in course design involve the breaking down of the course into units of study, which is often related to the use of textbooks.
