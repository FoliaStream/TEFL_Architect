**Equality vs. Hierarchy**

Cultures differ in their attitude towards social status. In a culture based on equality, people tend to be treated equally regardless of their position, gender, or age. On the other hand, in a culture based on hierarchy, people are usually assigned different roles on the basis of their social position. The social status may be a result of someone's age, educational background, or gender. People with a lower position will follow directions from those with a higher position.

A common stereotype of the culture of the United States is that it is based on equality (in theory if not always in practice). Ideally, both genders should be treated in the same way. An older person is not always to be respected more just because of their age, but it is better to evaluate a person's individual achievements. At work, Americans tend to be more informal with their superiors and treat them more as equals, expecting to be treated as equals in return.

Japanese culture, on the other hand, has some elements based on hierarchy. In general, the older someone is, the more respected that person should be. Men and women are assigned different roles and are expected to behave differently and be treated differently. A person whose status is lower is unlikely to challenge the opinions of those who are higher.

Equality vs. hierarchy is also reflected in the language people use, and it has important implications for the teacher-student relationship.

**Language:** In English, there is only one word for the second-person subject pronoun: "you." Regardless of the person's status or whether or not you know him or her well, "you" will always be used. In other languages, however, there may be several different words for the second person subject pronoun that are used according to the person's social status.

French, for example, has two different second person subject pronouns: tu and vous. Both of them can be translated into English as you, but if you address your friend, relative, or a child, you should use the word tu, while if you are talking to someone older than you, or someone you don't know well, you must use the word vous. The same distinction can be found in German and Turkish, where there are two forms of you: du and Sie, and sen and ski, respectively. Sometimes there are more than two words for you. Thai, for example, has 14 different forms of addressing another person depending on their social position and age.

**Classroom Implications:** In the United States, teachers and students are generally treated equally. Classes are more student-centered, learners are expected to ask questions if they do not understand something, and students are encouraged to share their opinions with the teacher. In an EFL classroom, there is an emphasis on limiting teacher talking time and increasing student participation.

By contrast, students in Japan are often considered culturally to have lower standing than the teacher. They are accustomed to not interrupting or challenging the instructor. Classes are much more teacher-centered, where the teacher lectures and initiates all classroom conversations. Students are often not accustomed to sharing their opinions or participating in class discussions.

Teaching in Asia can be very surprising for many American teachers. Students may not be accustomed to asking many questions and/or to talking without explicit instruction to do so. Judging just this behavior and not understanding the values behind it, teachers from the United States may assume that Asian students do not like them, while the truth is that they are trying to be respectful to the teacher. See Table 1.

**Table 1. Equality vs. hierarchy in the classroom.**

| Equality    | Hierarchy    |
|---|---|
| • Student-centered    | • Teacher-centered    |
| • Teachers and students are equals    | • Teachers are treated with respect or even fear    |
| • Students ask questions and may interrupt the teacher if they do not understand something | • Students do not ask questions and never interrupt the teacher |
| • Students share their opinions    | • Teacher initiates all classroom interaction |

Adapted from Peterson (2004)

**Solutions:** When preparing for a class in a culture based on hierarchy, plan for lots of pair- and group-work activities, as your students will be more comfortable talking in peer groups than with you, their teacher. If you teach adults, though, keep in mind and have caution with peer groups of different cultural hierarchies. You may need to practice working across the differences before everyone is comfortable interacting as they would in a homogenous group with respect to power positions.

Make sure you check your students' understanding not by asking a “yes/no” question (as the answer will always be “yes”), but rather by asking them to restate what you said or to give an example of what they have to do.

Finally, accept that your role may come with cultural implications that should be respected. Do not try to become too informal with your students because you may lose their respect, which may result in discipline problems.
