**Individual vs Group**

In *individual cultures*, people are viewed as individuals; they are expected to take initiative, focus on themselves, and put the well-being of an individual before a group’s interests. People also tend to make friends faster but more superficially, and are comfortable moving in and out of groups.

In *group or collective cultures*, people belong to a certain group and their identity is determined by the group affiliation. Their decisions are made by all group members, and not individually. The interests of a group are more important than the well-being of an individual.

In societies that are more group-based (e.g., most Asian or Arab countries), family usually plays a paramount role, such that sometimes adult children are hesitant to make a decision without their parents’ consent. It is common for married couples to live together with their parents in one house or at least nearby.

In contrast, the United States is a country that culturally focuses on individualism, not collectivism. In many homes in the United States, newborns are immediately expected to sleep in their own crib, a **practice** not done by many Asians that co-sleep with their children in the same bed until a child is four or five years old. In Italy, three generations may live under the same roof, and in some areas, Italian parents must be asked for permission before their adult children can marry. The United States practices an individual-based society, so many citizens are taught to make decisions without considering the collective’s opinions and beliefs.

**Classroom Implications:** In most individual cultures, it is expected that students study and do their own work. There is an emphasis on being self-reliant, and that is why serious consequences can follow if a student cheats on a test or plagiarizes an assignment.

In group societies, the expectation is often that students study in order to learn how to do things. The concept of continuous learning can be less important because everyone has an assigned role within a group, which makes changing professions or receiving a promotion much less common. Overall, cooperation is more highly valued; therefore, plagiarizing or using outside help are not always considered as harmful things to do. For these reasons, teaching in some Asian countries may be a challenge for an American teacher who is used to the different standards of the United States, where academic dishonesty and plagiarism are defined—and can be viewed—quite differently. See Table 3.

---

**Table 3. Individual vs. group cultures in the classroom**

| Individual Cultures    | Group Cultures    |
|---|---|
| Emphasis on learning how to study.    | Emphasis on learning how to do things.    |
| Idea of continuous learning.    | Most people study when they are young and rarely continue into adulthood.    |
| Students helping one another on an exam is not acceptable.    | Teachers from turn a blind eye to students who help each other during an exam or copy outside sources word-for-word.    |
| Plagiarism (the word-for-word use of an outside source) is unethical and even criminal.    |    |

---

**Solutions:**

Review and define the concept of academic honesty at the onset of class when communicating classroom policies and consequences. Reiterate points about avoiding academic dishonesty and plagiarism when the opportunity arises. Consider giving group projects instead of individual testing. If you must assess individual students, consider the cultural standard for assistance on tasks. Give clear instructions to your students about why this is individual work and provide examples of behaviors in your definition of cheating. Consider using open-ended questions so that it is harder for students to replicate each other’s answers. Do not get angry with your students if your testing rules pose a challenge, as it is probably not their intention to be disrespectful; the behavior may be due to the cultural differences in how we define academic honesty.
