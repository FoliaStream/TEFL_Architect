**Task vs. Relationship**

In cultures based on a *task style*, work is a very important part of a person’s life. Their job defines who they are and people are willing to sacrifice their leisure or family time in favor of work. They do business before pleasure. But in cultures based on a *relationship style*, work is still considered important, but relationships and family are imperative. A job is just a job and it does not define who the person is. Instead, family and relationships define the person (e.g., “I am the oldest brother”).

American culture tends to be task-based. If someone receives a good job offer, they may move to a different state or even country, leaving their extended family behind. Young people who go to college usually do so in a different city than where their parents live. This is because American culture is highly individual and generally task-oriented. Prioritizing business over pleasure is the common. Arab culture, on the other hand, is more relationship-oriented. Family tends to be the primary consideration, and conducting business requires the establishment of relationships first.

**Classroom Implications:** When teaching in more relationship-oriented cultures, such as in Arab, Indian, or Latino cultures, do not be surprised if a student is not in from the class for a week or longer if something concerning their family occurs; it may be the birth of a cousin, a wedding ceremony, or a grandparent’s illness. Students from such cultures give priority to family loyalty, even if it might negatively affect their school performance. See Table 4.

**Table 4. Task vs. relationship cultures in the classroom**

| Task Cultures    | Relationship Cultures    |
|---|---|
| Classroom attendance is essential.    | Absenteeism may result from family obligations and is more accepted overall.    |

**Solutions:** Make your classroom expectations clear, but at the same time try not to become frustrated with a prolonged absence of one of your students. Always save handouts for the students who are not in class. Be empathetic and ask if they need help with the covered material. You can assign another student to help the person who was absent. Do not teach the same lesson again; instead, ask the students who were present to explain what you covered in class.

Finally, make sure that you explain to your students that if they ever decide to study or work in the United States, classroom attendance is expected. Being absent from class or work is permitted but it is standard to seek approval in advance.
