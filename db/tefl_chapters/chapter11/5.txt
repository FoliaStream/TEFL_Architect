**Language Barrier**

The first barrier is language. Often, if we do not speak the language of a host country, we will encounter many situations in which it will be hard to communicate. However, it is important to realize that learning a new language not only consists of studying vocabulary and grammar. It also includes cultural competence: “Knowing what to say, and how, when, where and why to say it,” may sometimes be more important than using correct grammar (Hofstede et al., 2002, p. 18). To get you thinking more about this topic, read the following teacher vignette:

**Vignette: My daughter is not beautiful!**

In one advanced-level EFL conversation class in an East Asian country, I was teaching students how to politely accept and reject requests and compliments on a wide variety of topics. During one paired role play activity, a student drew a card that read, *You are in the hospital visiting a friend because they just had a baby girl (she your friend’s compliment on the new baby.* Thus, the student turned to her partner and said, *Your new baby girl is adorable.* Students who had been instructed to accept the compliment, looked confused, gave a long pause, and then shook his head, saying, “No, No…my daughter is not beautiful!” As the teacher, I was thoroughly confused by his response.

A little back-and-forth with the students taught me that in their culture it is considered beautiful to accept compliments about oneself. Since family is considered an extension of oneself, compliments on family should be also rejected.

This misunderstanding reminded me of how intertwined language and culture really are.

As you can see from the story above, culture has a very important consequence for an EFL classroom: When we teach English to learners coming from different cultures, we should always address observable cultural differences. For example, when you teach the phrase “How are you?” you need to teach that this is just a greeting – it is not a real question about how someone is. A native English speaker will be very surprised if the other person, instead of saying “good” or “great”, starts to complain about money or health problems. You do not expect somebody to tell you what worries them when you use this greeting.

Similarly, do not assume this is the case in other languages. If you ask the question, “How are you?” to a Hungarian, Russian, or Pole, they provide more detail beyond a one-word “good” answer. It is not appropriate in their culture to be so positive. Why would you say something that is not true? Why would you boast? In many cultures, if you ask this question, it means that you really want to know how the other person is feeling. If you were not interested in their feelings, you would not ask the question.

In Arab culture, there is yet another difference. It may not be enough just to ask “How are you?” when greeting another person. You should also ask about the person’s relatives, so “How is your family?” would be the next question. Again, most people the U.S. do not expect people that they do not know well to ask about family, but this is not necessarily true in other cultures.

Teaching the nuances of language means teaching cultural competence, and this is a very important aspect of any EFL class as it will contribute to students’ success in real-life situations. Therefore, if a student ever says or does something that seems culturally inappropriate to you, do not take offense, and never take it personally. Ask for clarification and view it as a teachable moment. It may be something that is appropriate in their culture, and the student has simply translated it into English.
