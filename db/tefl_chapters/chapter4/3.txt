**Language Levels**

When lesson planning, one of the first questions to consider is the language level of your students. Commonly called proficiency level(s), you saw in chapter 1 that English language learners are typically grouped into categories of beginner, intermediate, and advanced. There are also several possible subsets of these categories, such as "low," "mid," and "high" (as in high-beginner, mid-intermediate, low-advanced, etc.). Keep in mind that different institutions can use various terminology for these subsets, but most follow these basic descriptions. The National Reporting System (NRS), for example, uses six levels to classify language learners: Beginning Literacy, Low-Beginner, High-Beginner, Low-intermediate, High-Intermediate, and Advanced.

For most schools and language institutions, levels are defined by skill ability, meaning the things that a student who has attained that level is able to do with their additional language. For that reason, most descriptors of language level start by using the SWBAT format:

At this level of proficiency, **students will be able to...**

*Listening Indicators*
Learners may demonstrate listening comprehension non-verbally, and **will be able to...**

- Recognize letters of words when spelled or dictated
- Recognize/respond to greetings and polite expressions in **routine** and familiar settings
- Recognize/respond to simple questions/statements in familiar contexts (e.g., weather, family, employment)
- Follow one-step directions in a familiar context to complete a simple **task**
- Identify simple expressions indicating lack of understanding
- Recognize/respond to requests for repetition
- Recognize/respond to personal information

When relating levels and indicators to individuals or classes, it is important to remember that some students are more proficient in some **language skills** than others. For example, you might encounter a student who displays advanced reading and writing skills, but low-intermediate speaking and listening skills. It is also important to remember that student placement practices vary by school, which means that you could end up with students who have very similar proficiency levels in the same class, or you could have a wide range of skill proficiencies in a class. Learning the range in proficiencies of your students via a **needs analysis** at the onset of your course will help you to plan and manage your lesson planning (see chapter 10 for a sample needs analysis).
