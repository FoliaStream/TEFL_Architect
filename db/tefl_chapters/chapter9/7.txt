**Pronunciation**

In the history of **ESL/FIL** pedagogy, the teaching of pronunciation has gone from no emphasis at all to an over-fixation, but under the prevailing **communicative approach**, pronunciation is seen as an integral component. As a concept, “proper” pronunciation is problematic because there is no single correct accent when it comes to the English language. Furthermore, improvement in this area takes time and **practice**, and most FIL students will almost always speak with what native speakers would call an accent. As teachers, we want our students to improve pronunciation so that their speech is comprehensible to others. To that end, this section will provide an overview of pronunciation features and instruction while providing resources for further exploration.

**Pronunciation Features**

The field of pronunciation is complex and has many concepts and terms that are outside the scope of this book. Speaking and listening skills are intrinsically linked, and the two skills share many acquisition factors, so some of the content from chapter 8 is applicable here as well. For the sake of concision, see Appendix A of this chapter for expanded explanations of *voicing stress*, *rhythm*, *intonation*, and *reductions*. For now, let’s consider two concepts that are related to the teaching of pronunciation and that EFL teachers need to be familiar with: Segmental vs. suprasegmental practice and the International Phonetic Alphabet.

**Segmental vs. suprasegmental practice**

Two important terms in pronunciation practice are *segmental* and *suprasegmental*. **Segmental** pronunciation practice entails working with the individual *phonemes* (i.e., sounds) of vowels and consonants. If you teach your students how to sound out a letter in the alphabet or a single sound within a word, you are building segmental awareness in English pronunciation. On the other hand, **suprasegmental** pronunciation practice extends over more than one sound to have students work with chunks of language (e.g., stress, rhythm, intonation, pitch, linking, pausing, and thought groups). Teaching students to lower their voice on the end of a _wh_-question, placing stress on appropriate syllables/words, or practicing short versus long vowels are all examples of practices that build suprasegmental awareness.

**International Phonetic Alphabet**

The International Phonetic Alphabet (or IPA), commonly presented in a chart like the one in Figure 1, is a visual representation of all of the individual (or segmental) sounds that exist in the English language. It can be a handy reference for both students and teachers to show the pronunciation of new and unfamiliar sounds or words, one that your students may or may not be familiar with. Keep in mind that while language professionals tend to use the International Phonetic Alphabet, many reference works and dictionaries use transcription systems other than IPA.
