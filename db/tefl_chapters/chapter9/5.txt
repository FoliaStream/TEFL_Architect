**Tips for Teaching Speaking**

When focusing on oral skills in the classroom, we must avoid the **practice** of speaking for speaking’s sake. Students expect structure in their lessons, and open discussion without any context, specific learning goals, or preparation is generally ineffective. Along those lines, Bologna & Folse (2003) found that **ESL/EFL** learners produce more accurate language when the following occurs:

- The activity is multi-directional and communicative (i.e., requiring an exchange of actual information between two or more participants).
- The activity allows students _time to plan_ their output (i.e., what they are going to say, which means providing students with clear instructions on what the **task** requires and perhaps a span of time to prepare).
- Practice activities are closed tasks, meaning that they often have a finite set of possible answers.
- Follow-up **production** activities are more open-ended, meaning that students often have a variety of independent ways to achieve the communication goal(s).

It is also important to consider students’ age when incorporating speaking tasks into a lesson plan. Output expectations in practice and production activities should be lower for younger students. Very young students may only be ready for very simple listen-and-drill speaking activities or basic **songs**. As students age and mature, teachers may begin to incorporate more of the speaking activities that are described in the following section.
