**Student Abilities and Realistic Goals**

Perhaps more than with any other skill or subset of English instruction, **EFL** learners have wide ranges of ability in this area, meaning that some students need additional teaching for forming correct and intelligible sounds, while others need very little. A great number of influences are involved in their **acquisition** of good pronunciation habits and of the second language in general. Some of these factors will be beyond your control, but your role as a teacher is to be aware of these issues, the degree to which they affect your classroom environment and student performance, and how to compensate for them when and where possible. Listed below are some of the key factors that affect a learner’s ability to acquire good second language pronunciation.

- **Age:** It is commonly assumed that someone who acquires a second language later in life will not be able to **approach**—let alone master – native-like pronunciation, compared to someone who learns one at a young age. Linguists have struggled with this question for a long time, but what we do know is that with age, physical changes take place in the brain (e.g., slower formation of new neural pathways) and that this may affect the ability to acquire second language knowledge and habits.
- **Learner’s First Language (L1):** Students often overlap their native language sound system on the pronunciation of English, which accounts for a learner’s characteristic accent. The lack of certain phonological features in the first language that are present in English are an area of improvement (e.g., native Thai speakers are often challenged by English consonant clusters). Additionally, as you learned in chapter 8, this gap in sounds may interfere with the learner’s ability to distinguish sounds not present in their native language. If you have a class who all share the same first language, familiarity yourself with the sound features of that language to gain an understanding of where your students are coming from (Lane & Brown, 2010; Nation & Newton, 2009; Kenworthy, 1987).
- **Amount of Second (Target) Language Exposure** and User Acquisition of a language is strongly correlated to the amount of exposure to the target language. As a teacher, try to maximize students’ exposure to authentic models of the language and provide ample opportunities and activities to **practice**, both inside and especially outside of class (Celce-Murcia, et al., 2010; Lane & Brown, 2010; Kenworthy, 1987).

This short list above only touches on some of the factors that affect second language acquisition. However, they are enough to demonstrate the complexity involved in learning pronunciation. You’ll want to avoid aiming for students to have perfect mastery of English pronunciation—it may not be practical or even possible.

*Setting Realistic Goals*

Students may feel that they need to sound exactly like a native speaker, but that is not something that most should strive toward. Only a small percentage of second language learners are able to sound like a native speaker of English, and native-like pronunciation is generally the last skill acquired. Instead, strive for comprehensible and intelligible **production:** If they are able to communicate their messages and intentions accurately in the second language, they have achieved success (Celce-Murcia, et al., 2010; Lane & Brown, 2010; Folse, 2006; Nation & Newton, 2009; Kenworthy, 1987). Furthermore, having an accent is unlikely to prevent them from achieving their long-term language goals. There are many individuals who are very successful in their fields and still retain their native language accent. You should make your students aware of this fact.

Again, it is most important that your students communicate with and be understood by their audience. If they meet those criteria, they have successfully communicated their message, regardless of their accent. Whatever goals your students set, make sure they are explicit, realistic, and attainable. Addressing the above points will go a long way toward making your students comprehensible and intelligible to a listener, especially if they are at a lower level of speaking proficiency.
