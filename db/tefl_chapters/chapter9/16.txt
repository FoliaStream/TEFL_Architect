**The Process Approach to Writing**

Currently, the dominant trend in **ESL/EFL** writing is the process approach. As opposed to the product approach, where students are expected to produce a written response to a task in one take, the process approach asks that students actively engage the task using a step-by-step series of writing stages, involving multiple drafts and revisions, with feedback given at each stage. Student writing, therefore, goes through a process of evolution and growth. The process approach can be used for any type of writing assignment or task genre, and can be outlined as follows:

*1. Pre-Writing/Planning*

Students begin the process by generating ideas for the task:
  - **Brainstorming**: In pairs or groups, students generate ideas about the topic, recording them as they discuss. This allows them to take advantage of their collective knowledge, and they are free to use any and all ideas in their writing.
  - **Listing**: Individually, students generate a list of ideas about the topic, as many as possible. Introverted students may respond to this technique more positively.
  - **Cluster Diagram**: This is also known as a mind map. Students begin by placing a keyword or main idea in a central circle, then branching out with related ideas, eventually forming a pattern. This is an effective technique for students who are visual learners. 
  - **Frewwriting**: Students write down as many ideas that come to mind as quickly as possible, preferably within a time limit. Students use the resulting material as a springboard for their writing task.

Try different techniques with your students and have them stick with one that fits their particular learning style. Regardless of which technique they use, your role at this point is minimal, so hold off on any feedback or corrections. The point here is to allow students the freedom to generate ideas without judgment or interference.

---

*2. First Draft*

Once the student has generated ideas, they write the first draft of the task. **Stress to** the student that it need not be perfect at this point and that they will have an opportunity to improve their writing as the process continues. The purpose here is to begin organizing pre-writing ideas into a coherent and cohesive whole.

--- 

*3. Peer Review*

Students exchange and read each other’s first drafts, then come up with comments and suggestions for the writer. Typically, the teacher provides a list of areas to focus on and provide commentary (e.g., content, organization, grammar, etc.). Some students may be resistant as they feel that their peers (or they themselves) do not have the authority or language skills to offer genuine feedback. Stress to them that they do have something valuable to contribute, and the wrong thing is part of the writing process will help them truly learn writing skills. They can locate and identify problem areas, then they understand the underlying concepts, which they can then bring into their own writing.

Also, make sure to let students know that any suggestions and advice should be given constructively and politely. Provide them with a framework of what to look for in their peers’ writing. For example, if the product involves creating a good topic sentence, have students focus on that aspect instead of any other errors.

---

*4. Second Draft*

Students use the feedback from the peer review session to write an improved version of the task, which is then submitted to the teacher for review prior to final evaluation.

---

*5. Editing and Revision*

The teacher provides feedback to the writer on the second draft, checking all language areas focused on the particular assignment. The teacher should strive to offer fair but honest feedback and to be positive whenever possible. Avoid too much over-correction and red ink and ignore language errors that are not part of the domain of the assignment.

---

*6. Final Drft Product*

The student uses the teacher’s feedback from the second draft to write a final version. This is the version that will be formally evaluated by the teacher using a rubric with clear criteria that have been communicated to the writer.

---

Overall, the writing process approach has been shown to have many benefits in terms of teaching second language writing by:

- Allowing students some degree of ownership over the assigned task.
- Helping students internalize concepts of English language and writing.
- Encouraging cooperation, collaboration, and cohesion among students.
- Giving students ample time and opportunities to improve the final written product.
