**Tips for Teaching Pronunciation**

Like any other component of language teaching, the teacher must decide when and where to include pronunciation activities in the scope of lessons. A number of options are available, as outlined below.

✔ **Devoting an entire lesson to pronunciation:** Teachers may do this on their own or as part of a set schedule. It may not make sense to focus on only one or two sounds for a 45- to 60-minute period, so work on extended suprasegmental features such as **rhythm**, **intonation**, and **stress**, and implement them as part of longer **discourse** structure **practice**. You can also integrate other related skills (e.g., listening or vocabulary) prior to actual speech **production**.

✔ **Using discrete slots of time:** Many teachers choose to insert short, manageable chunks of pronunciation activities into individual lessons. This can be useful for helping students achieve individually set goals and tasks. It can also be a nice change of pace from the usual lesson structure, and students tend to enjoy this aspect when it is worked in alongside other language aims.

✔ **Integrated practice:** The teacher may have students focus on a speech feature that correlates to a language point, such as listening for an aspect of pronunciation present in a recording or asking them to use proper intonation when practicing question structures. Draw their attention to features of accurate and authentic speech models.

✔ **Opportunities that present themselves:** Also referred to as "teachable moments," the teacher may deviate from the planned lesson sequence, often in response to an opportunity that presents itself on an individual level. Care must be taken not to get sidetracked from the overall goal of the lesson, so avoid spending more than a few minutes in this manner.

The best **approach** is an eclectic mix of all these techniques. Mix and match the options above and tailor them to the needs of your students and class. Keep the following general tips in mind:

- Do a **needs analysis** and pronunciation profile of your students at the onset of a course (see chapter 10 for more on needs analysis).
- Help your students develop self-awareness of their spoken production and set realistic goals.
- Incorporate pronunciation activities into your lessons as needed, schedule them regularly, and practice a few minutes daily.
- Practice both individual sounds and prosodic features (i.e., segmental and suprasegmental pronunciation) using a variety of activities.
- Like other **language skills**, remind your students that improving their pronunciation requires focused practice inside _and_ outside of class.
- The overall goal should be intelligible, comprehensible output, not perfect speech.

Above all, make your pronunciation lessons fun and energetic. Enjoying the lessons and feeling comfortable taking risks will go a long way toward ensuring that your students make steady progress.
