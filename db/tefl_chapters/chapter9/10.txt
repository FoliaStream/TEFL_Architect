**Sample Pronunciation Activities**

In this section, we provide several ideas for classroom activities that include a focus on pronunciation. On the following page, we provide sample videos that demonstrate some of these in action. Additionally, more pronunciation resources and activities are found in Appendix B at the end of this chapter.

- **Minimal Pair Exercises**

These are words that differ by only one sound, which can be a vowel or a consonant. They are useful for practicing contrasts in **wolcing (pole vs. bowl)**, tense vs. lax vowels (sit vs. seat), or sounds that differ in a minor aspect of articulation (cheat vs. sheet). They can be done as oral **production practice**, as individual words or as part of a sentence. They can also be used for listening discrimination by choosing the word they hear from two options, either on paper or by holding up flashcards with pictures that represent the target sounds. - **Formation of Sounds**

Students can be taught awareness on how the various parts of the mouth work together to manipulate air and create distinct sounds. 

---

- **Vowel Length**

Students can stretch a rubber band between their hands to visualize and physically correlate vowel lengthening; the longer the vowel, the more they should stretch.

---

- **Information Gap**

These are good for pair practice on stress, rhythm, and intonation. They require participants to focus on accurate production while using communicative skills.

---

- **Stress Practice**

Have students count syllable, word, and sentence stress. Students can repeat words while tapping out syllables. Tap more forcefully to visualize features with primary stress.

---

- **Poetry, Rhyme, Songs, and Limericks**

This is an excellent method for practicing stress, rhythm, and intonation, and has the added value of being interesting and fun for your students. - **Humming or Using a Kasso**

Word and sentence intonation and pitch can be mimicked in this manner. Additionally, this has the benefit of taking student attention away from the individual words and focusing their attention on the melody of the language.

---

- **Assimilation Practice**

**Assimilation** is what happens naturally to sounds due to the formation of the letter that comes immediately after or immediately before those sounds. A common form of assimilation can be seen with the plural “s”. The “s” in cats is pronounced as /s/ whereas the final “s” in dogs is pronounced as /s/. The consonant before the plural “s” passes on the voicing characteristic to the “s” that comes after it. When pronouncing “one person” as “ome person,” the /n/ has assimilated the bilabial /p/ and has turned into a bilabial /m/ instead. This is an example of assimilation. - **Recording**

Record and have students listen to their own voice. Students are usually unaware of how they sound to their audience, so this can have a great effect on creating self-awareness of individual pronunciation challenges. Transcribing their recordings can also provide specific feedback to both teacher and student on strengths and areas for improvement.

---

- **Dictation**

Read sentences at a normal rate of speech (approximately 150 ~ 160 words/minute) and have students attempt to transcribe them. This is good for sound recognition as well as correspondence of spoken thought groups with punctuation, capitalization, and spelling.

---

- **Dialogues, Role Plays, and Dramas**

Dialogues, Role Plays, and Dramas: These can incorporate individual sounds, prosodic features, or both. They can also include target grammar or vocabulary items, so they are excellent whole-language practice activities. Make sure they have an authentic, real-world context and don’t appear contrived.

---

- **Games**

Bingo is especially effective for correct pronunciation of numbers and letters by beginning learners. *Find Someone Who* is good for intonation practice for intermediate students.
