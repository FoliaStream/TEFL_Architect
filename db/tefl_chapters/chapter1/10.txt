**Addressing Student Behavioral Issues**

Despite your best efforts as a teacher, there will be times when things get out of hand in the classroom. All teachers have had to confront a student who presents a behavioral issue at some point, and it is not too early to start thinking about strategies for addressing behavioral issues. How you handle and approach these issues will determine whether you can successfully deal with the issue and the overall effect it will have on the classroom environment.

- First, determine what the issue is. There may be an underlying reason for the behavior the student is exhibiting. If you can make that determination, you may be able to resolve the issue immediately.
- Deal with the behavior as soon as it appears. Waiting to act may only make it worse and more difficult to resolve later.
- Remain calm and focused. Be proactive, not reactive. Behave professionally, and don’t let things get to you personally.
- Focus on the students’ actions, not the student. Keep any criticism detached and impersonal. Avoid any type of personal commentary.
- Be forward-looking. Try to resolve issues in a positive manner, and once the issue has been handled, continue with the lesson and let it go.
- If you must speak to a student, do so privately. It’s generally unproductive to confront a student in front of his or her classmates, especially within certain cultures. Talking one-on-one is always better.
- Enforce your class rules as agreed upon. If you’ve taken the time during the first week to establish policies, procedures, and consequences for violating them, any disciplinary actions will come as no surprise to the student. Chapter 5 will help you to consider some specific policies and how to include them in your course documents.
- If all else fails, consult with your colleagues and program administrators. Your co-teachers, especially experienced ones, may have valuable suggestions to give you in these situations. On the rare occasion when you have a student that is particularly recalcitrant, speak to your program administrators. Chances are that they have dealt with similar issues and can provide you with advice and support.

It is important to follow through with your actions. For example, if you tell a student that s/he has to stay after school if s/he does not stop talking out of turn, make sure that you are able to follow through with this plan. If you are not allowed to keep students after class due to bus schedules or other reasons, then find another disciplinary action or consequence. Remember that if you say one thing and do another, your students will not respect anything that you say because they know your follow-through is inconsistent. It is easy to loosen up later if you are too strict in the first weeks. It is not as easy to become more stringent if you are too lenient at the beginning.
