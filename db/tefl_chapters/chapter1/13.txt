**The Teaching Process**

The teaching process is complex. Many researchers and theorists will note that it is hard to get at the essence of good teaching, or the “nuts and bolts” of teaching, because so much in the classroom happens extemporaneously (Ellis, 2003). The mix of teacher and student personalities, curriculum, and environmental factors are things that cannot be predicted before someone steps into a particular classroom environment. It always helps to see examples of strong and weak teaching practices.

There are many places where you can look for examples of effective teaching, including various news reports, articles, blogs, and books on the subject. Consider observing seasoned teachers in action. You will see that there are several things that make a teacher successful. For instance, high-performing teachers will strive to move the whole class to a higher level of language competence without leaving students behind. They will also try to make things fun and think outside of the box, perhaps by thinking of innovative, interactive ways to do potentially boring grammar tasks. They want to make sure that they have methods like comprehension checks to help reassess where everyone is and see if everyone has really understood what was taught. Many teachers prefer to ask questions to elicit responses to monitor progress. These teachers will go back and reteach or continue to practice the concepts until the entire class gets it, all while maintaining curriculum needs and class schedules.

Effective teachers are also flexible with their lesson plans. The lesson plan is just that a plan you make before you go into the classroom. However, like all plans in life, they might change. Thus, high-quality teachers are prepared to vary their plans to better address student needs. As Ellis (2003) states, much of what constitutes good teaching happens spontaneously in the classroom. You will have spontaneous ideas or react to a particular classroom environment, and a lesson plan cannot possibly cover every unforeseen problem or student question that you come across. With that in mind, lesson planning should be viewed as a guide to help keep your teaching and classroom activities on track. The good news is that the more you teach, the more adaptive and effective planner you become.

*Ineffective vs. Effective Teaching*: 

When thinking back to teachers from our past, most of us can remember teachers that were highly effective, as well as teachers that were much less effective or engaging. Doing this mental exercise may help you to put a finger on some classroom practices that did and did not work for you. With that in mind, the points below highlight best practices that can be implemented to overcome ineffective teaching habits. Click on each point to expand it for more information.

---

*Always try new things*

Ineffective teachers can often be “stuck in their ways.” Teaching is a dynamic and ever-changing experience. As such, teachers need to be willing to try new things. While a good teacher might reuse a good activity or project, almost no teacher would reuse the same exact lesson plan.

*Set the stage from the first moment*

How you begin class sets the stage for the entire experience. Many times, both new and experienced teachers will jump into new material without reviewing information from the previous class. However, each piece of knowledge builds on the last. Thus, the beginning of any class should begin with a review of what students already know before embarking on a new topic.


*Use class time effectively*

Ineffective teachers will design gaps in class time for their benefit (to do outside work or take a nap!). Effective teachers realize that every second of class time is worth its weight in gold, and they use it to the fullest. They are able to multi-task so that students are always engaged and never really notice that the teacher is completing other tasks while they are learning.


*Never leave your class*

It’s best to never leave a classroom full of students unless it is absolutely necessary. It allows you to maintain your focus and minimize the possibility that something could happen in your classroom that you did not know about. An ineffective teacher will leave the classroom to complete tasks such as retrieving copies or picking up a forgotten book. Leaving the classroom opens up the possibility of any number of undesired occurrences from having no one monitoring the environment. If you need something from somewhere else or need something given to an administrator or the office, request a volunteer, and only when absolutely necessary. Just make sure to only use students to complete these functions in limited capacities. You do not want to ask your students to get items that you could have brought to your classroom. You also do not want to have students bringing you coffee. Only have students assist with absolutely essential administrative errands.


*Understand technology*

Know how to use relevant technology. Many of us use computers and other electronic devices every day; however, we may not know how to maximize these resources for educational purposes. We can connect with other colleagues via our local professional organizations. We can also use the internet for instructional practices, which is one of the best ways to use it. Technology is constantly changing, and every day we encounter a new contribution to the technological world. For example, many teachers today use PowerPoint presentations, but there is new software and freeware that has already begun replacing older presentation tools. Always be prepared in case technology fails (e.g., you can't log on to the network or the internet is not working). These are issues that teachers face from time to time.


*Limit teacher talk time*

The ineffective teacher may lecture extensively, thinking that by covering everything, they are teaching everything. Not everything can be covered, so we need to pick out the most important points and focus on those. Furthermore, it has been argued that only a small percentage of what is learned in a lecture format is retained. We also know that students can only retain so much information in one sitting. Thus, by covering more material and speaking more, we may actually be hindering our learners from learning. Furthermore, class time is a great opportunity (often the only opportunity) for many EFL students to actively use English, and every minute that the teacher is talking is a minute that the students cannot.


*Manage your classroom*

A well-managed classroom needs a teacher to organize students, give concise and comprehensible directions, plan measurable objectives and goals that students can reach, and set clear time limits on each part of the lesson. Creative teachers know how to organize their lessons and their classroom in ways that motivate students and allow them to excel.


*Your peers are your best resources* 

Effective teachers talk to their colleagues. Whether you have an official mentor or learning team or these relationships develop in a de facto manner as your group interacts, you will always want to be actively encouraging these interactions. You will want to seek out peers, look for ways to meet outside of class, be sociable, and see if some of your colleagues already collaborate outside of school hours. Your experience can be greatly enhanced if you seek out people who can be professional resources for you.


*Avoid negativity*

Negativity can seep into any work environment. We can always approach any situation from a pessimistic viewpoint, but if we approach it with an optimistic viewpoint, things will run much more smoothly. If you notice that you are with people that engage in negative behavior, distance yourself and look for people who are happy with what they are doing. Do you want to be around people who never have anything good to say? Probably not.


*Be a facilitator* 

Many teachers believe that they give knowledge to their students, but this is not really the case. Instead, we help our students find and construct their own meaning by establishing a student-centered environment in which they can learn. We want our learners to acquire their knowledge through hands-on activities that prepare them for the real world and authentic use of the target language (English). Our goal is to be a facilitator of their learning as opposed to a giver of knowledge (which is related to LTTT from above).
