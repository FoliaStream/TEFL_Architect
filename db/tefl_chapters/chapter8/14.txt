**Types of Reading - Intensive and Extensive**

This section will review the two core types of reading that parallel the brain’s reading process and provide example practice activities.

*Intensive Reading*

Intensive reading requires reading for detail. It involves not only understanding vocabulary and the main idea of a passage but other skills as well, such as identifying inferences and understanding the author’s tone (humor, sarcasm, hyperbole, etc.) and purpose (to inform, to entertain, to persuade, etc.). Activities to practice intensive reading take many similar forms to those used in listening, including the following:

- True/false and yes/no questions for specific details
  - Why questions for specific details (open-ended or multiple choice)
- Error correction
  - Ticking, numbering, or reordering sentences
  - Filling out graphic organizers, forms, charts, and tables
- Skimming and scanning (addressed later in this chapter)
- Cloze (fill-in-the-blank) exercises

Because intensive reading is the most common type of reading skill practiced in a language classroom, there is more information and detail about its implementation throughout the rest of the chapter.

---

*Extensive Reading*

Extensive reading means that students are reading for pleasure. Note that the meaning of this term differs from the context of listening, where it means to listen for the general idea. Furthermore, unlike with listening, for which extensive and intensive skills can be practiced using the same audio recording, the practice of extensive and intensive reading skills requires different reading materials altogether. In reading, the term extensive refers to texts that are below level and based on student interest. Intensive reading texts, on the other hand, are above the students’ current level and are being read for purposes other than (but possibly in addition to) pleasure reading. Hence, the reading that we do in ESL/EFL classrooms is almost always intensive in nature, while extensive reading generally happens outside the classroom.

The extensive reading concept may sound simple, but it is an important part of becoming a strong reader. Research has shown that encouraging students to read for pleasure outside of class improves their vocabulary and grammatical knowledge (Bourret, 2009). Additionally, extensive reading encourages a joy for reading. Given this, teachers should encourage students to read texts that are at or below level for extensive reading, as above-level texts might frustrate or demotivate students from reading on their own. Activities to practice extensive reading can take many forms, including the following:

- Providing level-appropriate book options for reading outside of class (teacher- or student-selected)
- Providing reading packets that include level-appropriate newspaper articles and/or short stories based on students’ interests
- Having students select articles or stories of their choice every week

If you are selecting extensive readings for your students, it is helpful to determine in advance which topics might be appropriate for the students. To do this, you might survey the class and find that, for example, sports and fitness, movies and entertainment, and travel are the three dominant topics of interest among your students. You can then include articles and stories connected to each topic, thus appealing to each sector of the class. Furthermore, students of all ages may be interested in graphic novels, which provide engaging visuals that will likely enhance comprehension. One way to ensure that students are doing extensive reading tasks is to have them keep reader response journals. However, as the teacher, you need not grade these journals or mark them for mechanics. Instead, respond to the content by asking questions or making content-appropriate comments (Dunkelblau, 2007). Though allowing students to pick topics that appeal to them individually is ideal, it may not always be appropriate or practical for every learning situation (Hetherington, 1985). Thus, always be aware of course requirements and the level of student interest in this approach.
