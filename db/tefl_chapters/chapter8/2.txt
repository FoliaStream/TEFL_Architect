**Listening Processing**

Before getting into the specific strategies and techniques for the teaching of listening, there are some key terms that will help you better understand your students’ experiences with listening and how the brain generally processes aural input.

*Bottom-up Processing*

**Definition** – In this process, students focus on specific parts of the language they hear. To do this, they may focus on things such as keywords, structures, or specific sound features. This process can be thought of as language-focused rather than meaning-focused.

**Classroom Implications** – When running a listening task in the ESL/ERL classroom, this process is often practiced through having students listen for specific “details” of what they will hear, such as grammar, vocabulary, or pronunciation features. This is particularly beneficial for beginning-level students because of its focus on listening at the word and sentence level. This type of listening is often referred to as *intensive listening*.

*Top-down Processing*

**Definition** – In this process, students focus on the holistic meaning of the language they hear. To do this, they tap into what they already know about the topic or situation to aid in understanding the overall meaning of the language they hear. This process can be thought of as meaning-focused rather than language-focused.

**Classroom Implications** – When running a listening task in the ESL classroom, this process is often practiced through pre-listening tasks that trigger students’ background knowledge. It can also be practiced through prediction and guessing tasks as well as asking students to listen for the gist, main ideas, or setting. This type of listening is often referred to as *extensive listening*.

For effective second language acquisition to occur, students must be given activities that address both of these ways of processing aural information. When both processes take place together, it is called *interactive processing*. Interactive processing is something that you do naturally in your first language. For example, you predict what people will say based on the situation you are in. You also listen for specific details to distinguish information you need to understand the language.

Listeners’ goals of this interactive process vary. For example, the listener might tune into a speaker in order to understand how to perform an action or solve a problem. Conversely, the listener’s goals might simply be for enjoyment or pleasure (e.g., listening to music). Regardless of listener’s goals, use the principles of top-down and bottom-up processing in designing activities and planning lessons and curricula. Keeping those principles in mind will ensure that classroom time is used for maximum effectiveness, regardless of your students’ language level.
