**Choosing Materials and Formats**

There are multiple factors to consider in choosing a text. For example, we have already seen that limited background knowledge on a topic may interfere with reading comprehension. Assigning readings that are too simple or not age-appropriate may demotivate students. On the other hand, even if students have background knowledge on a subject, if the reading passage has too many unfamiliar words, they will be frustrated. Research suggests that around 95% of the text needs to contain brown words, or around 7 new words on a page of A4 size. In order for students to understand and guess the general meaning of unknown text (Nutrail, 2000, p.175). Given this, strive to select texts that match the readability level of your students.

Also, consider the complexity of the sentence structure of a text. Examine the readability of the following two passages. Which one is more grammatically complex?

*Stewart left the party early. Then, he went home to get some much-needed sleep. However, the neighbor’s dog barked all night. The noise kept him awake.*

*After leaving the party early, Stewart went home for some much-needed sleep but got none because the neighbor’s barking dog kept him awake all night.*

As you have probably guessed, the second passage is more complex, even though the meaning is the same as the first one. The first passage uses a predictable subject-verb-object pattern with clear transitional devices (then and however). The second passage is a complex sentence, consisting of an adverbial participle **clause**, as well as a main clause with one subject and a compound predicate (two verbs).

Another consideration is the concrete vs. abstract nature of the reading’s content. Readings focusing on abstract concepts such as liberty, love, or forgiveness are more difficult than readings dealing with concrete facts, such as dates, events, and other facts and figures. Even if a student has background knowledge and interest in abstract ideas such as “liberty,” passages focusing on such concepts will be harder if the student is very young or at the beginning or intermediate level (Hetherington, 1985).

To sum up how to choose a text for **ESL/FEL** learners, Hetherington (1985) provides a list of guiding questions to consider when selecting EFL/ESL readings:

1. Will this text interest my students?
2. Is there a meaningful purpose for reading this text?
3. Do my students have (or can I provide them with) the appropriate background knowledge to understanding the content?
4. Is the level of abstractions appropriate?
5. Is the passage complete in itself or has the author assumed other information or required inferencing skills?
6. What kind of extra-textual support is available?
7. Is the text clearly organized with a beginning or introduction and clear sequence signals?
8. Is there sufficient redundancy of ideas?
9. Will the number of difficult vocabulary items interfere with the assigned task?
10. Does the author use a lot of structures that are vague or difficult to process for students at this level?
11. Are syntactic relationships within sentences and between sentences clear?
12. Have I set an appropriate task for the type of text, the level of difficulty, and the needs of my students? Have I taught them the necessary skills to cope with the task?
