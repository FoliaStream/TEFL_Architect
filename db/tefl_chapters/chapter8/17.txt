**Teaching Reading at Different Levels**

In chapters 1 and 4, you were introduced to the topic of language levels, or *proficiency levels*, which included various charts and external resources with benchmark information on topics such as content, skills, and **accuracy** for each level. Figure 3 gives a snapshot of similar content in relation to reading, providing sample reading materials, topics, and skills for different levels.

*Figure 3.* ESL/EFL reading skills by level

|    | ESL/EFL Reading: Skills by Level |
|---|---|
| Level    | Materials/Reading Topics    | Sample Skills to Practice    |
| Beginner to Intermediate | ● Concrete and familiar topics (e.g., family, food, and school, with straightforward facts and figures; concrete verbs and descriptions) ● Simple syntax and vocabulary ● Highly contextualized with visuals and repetition of key points ● Functional readings appropriate for scanning, such as a local bus schedule, a travel brochure, a grocery store price list, class timetable, or current movie listings | ● Understanding main idea ● Scanning for specific information ● Guessing words from context ● Identifying discourse markers (which show cause/effect, sequence, contrast, etc.) ● Effective use of monolingual learners’ dictionaries ● Paraphrasing |
| Intermediate to Advanced | ● More abstract topics (figurative language more accessible to higher-level students; topics not necessarily obviously linked to students’ everyday lives) ● Gradual increase in syntax complexity and a wider vocabulary range ● Variety of sources acceptable if they meet these criteria | ● Analyzing writer’s point of view ● Identifying author’s tone and choice of rhetorical features ● Identifying purpose (to inform, to persuade, to entertain, etc.) ● Writing summaries of main points ● Making inferences |

The ideas presented in Figure 3 discuss how to effectively incorporate reading into lesson plans for adult learners. Many of these ideas can be adapted for young-learner classrooms by simplifying concepts and reading materials. Note, however, that unless you teach in a bilingual program, you will not likely be expected to use reading materials and activities for young learners until the students are able to read in their first language (typically starting between the ages of 5 and 7). Instead, reading instruction for young learners typically begins with an introduction to the alphabet and phonics and then moves into activities that incorporate simple sentences, paragraphs, or books in English. For more information on teaching reading skills to young learners, see International TEFL Academy’s *Teaching English to Young Learners* coursebook.
