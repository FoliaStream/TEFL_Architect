**Reading Processing**

Similar to the terms used in the context of listening, this section will examine the **bottom-up and top-down** processing of reading.

**Bottom-up Processing**

*Definition* - Bottom-up processing occurs when the reader “decodes” the word and sentence structure line by line. Here, the reader first deciphers the parts to understand the text as a whole. For example, we use a bottom-up **approach** when we look up unfamiliar words in the dictionary or attend to a writer’s use of punctuation to understand tone and style. We might also guess the meaning of a word by its part of speech or surrounding context.

*Classroom Implications* - When running a reading **task in the ESL/EFL** classroom, this process can be practiced through having students read for specific “details” of the text, such as grammar, vocabulary, or certain content. Other sample activities include identifying affixes or reading out loud in pairs. Researchers also refer to bottom-up processing as “data driven” (Haalley, 2001, p. 148).

**Top-down Processing**

*Definition* - Top-down processing occurs when the reader proceeds from a general idea of meaning to a more specific one. The reader reads a section, makes an internal hypothesis on the meaning of the text, and then reads more in order to either accept or reject previous hypotheses.

*Classroom Implications* - When running a reading task in the ESL classroom, this process is practiced in various ways. For example, you can have students predict what a text will be about based on titles, subtitles, pictures, etc. Other practices include having students annotate the text with comments (e.g., answering a question, writing a question, giving opinions). And finally, you might also have students read to identify main ideas in the text. Top-down processing is also known as “concept-driven” (Haalley, 2001, p. 148).

As in the processing of listening, a proficient reader likely performs a combination of all techniques when reading: bottom-up, top-down, as well as something called metacognition. The **metacognitive view** of reading describes a reader who thinks consciously about his or her reading strategies. A proficient reader will choose to scan, skim, use vocabulary clarification strategies, or read for detail as dictated by the purpose of the assignment (Poole, 2011). Students can be taught metacognitive skills through **intensive reading** lessons, which are discussed in more depth later in this chapter.
