**Common Student Difficulties**

In order to best assist your **EFL** students in reading, this section provides a brief, non-exhaustive list of common reading difficulties that language learners encounter.

*Students may lack background knowledge*

Gaps in reading comprehension are likely to occur when essential background information is missing. An important theory in reading, the **Schema** *theory* states that a reader brings in his or her own knowledge and experiences when trying to read a text. Therefore, a gap in “schema” may lower comprehension. This gap goes beyond one’s breadth of vocabulary to incorporate both social and cultural experiences (Stott, 2001). For example, **BLUEFL** readers are more likely to have trouble understanding text that has cultural-specific humor, stories, terms, or expressions and idioms.

To help bridge gaps in background knowledge, you should incorporate proper pre-reading tasks so that students are better equipped to guess meaning from the reading (Rumelhart, 1980). Several reading strategies that can be taught are referenced in a later section (entitled *Reading Sequenced*).

---

*Students may spend too much energy on individual words*

Language students tend to read much slower in their second language than in their first language (Fraser, 2007). It is not uncommon to see students labor over each word, tracking each word with a finger or a pencil or stop reading to look up and translate every unknown word in a dictionary (which should be discouraged). If students read in this slow manner, comprehension and pleasure for reading are lowered (Rutzall, 1996). Furthermore, if students are unable to decode the words at a fast enough rate, they will likely have to reread the text multiple times in order to comprehend what they are reading.

There are many ways to assist students in “decoding” words faster. For example, younger or beginning-level students might work on phonics for the alphabet or do activities with sight words (i.e., words that are learned by sight rather than by sounding out) to assist in reading faster at the word level. For higher-level students, you might incorporate speed reading or skimming practices that encourage students to read at a quicker pace. And finally, encouraging students to guess meaning through context (interencing) is an effective reading strategy that is especially relevant for top-down and extensive reading.

---

*Students may struggle with complex grammar structures*

In addition to difficulties with unknown words, students may struggle with a text in terms of grammatical or structural complexity. For example, longer sentences can mean that more clauses and phrases embedding can occur, adding a greater burden on the reader (Miller, 2011). Complex grammar or sentence structures not only affect comprehension but may also affect readers’ interests and responses to a text. Therefore, it is important to select texts that are grammatically and structurally level appropriate.

---

*Students may struggle with text structure*

Students who come from languages with written text structures different from that of English may have comprehension difficulties as a result. In English essay writing, for example, we often follow an introduction, body, and/or quotation structure. In some other languages, however, this type of structure does not exist and may be completely new to the students. Therefore, teaching your students to recognize text structures is likely to help them anticipate and better comprehend texts. For example, you can introduce outlines to your students to show the location of content in the reading. You might also use a graphic organizer to show sequence and/or what language is used to connect different pieces of information. Use of **graphic organizers** will be addressed in more depth in Chapter 10—Visual Aids and Technology.

---

*Students may struggle with motivation*

Regardless of how difficult or easy the text is, it will be difficult to read if it is of no interest to your students. Topic selection is a key factor in promoting strong readers and should not be overlooked. As mentioned elsewhere in the chapter, if suitable to the educational setting, you could conduct a **needs analysis** or administer a survey to learn about your students’ interests prior to topic selection.
