**Technology: Integrating Tools into the Classroom**

To begin a discussion on integrating technology into the EFL classroom, first read the following teacher vignette:

**Vignette: Lots of Sunshine, Few Resources**

I had always wanted to live in Latin America, so I was excited to move to Central America, take up the sunshine, dance to some good music, and work on my Spanish. After spending two years teaching in South Korea, however, I had become accustomed to classrooms equipped with smart boards, computers, fast internet, extra resources, etc.

My new teaching job had a chalkboard and shelf with construction paper and markers. There was no computer, but I could get spotty internet service by relieving from my smartphone to my own computer. To say the least, I had to reinvent the way I prepared and delivered my lessons. It took some time, creativity, and a few papercuts to become a fully “unplugged” EFL teacher. In the end, however, I found that I was able to be as successful, if not more so, in my minimalist classroom with just our own voices and some chalk and pencils to guide us.

The success that this teacher found in Central America shows us that access to technology in your classroom or school may give you many diverse visual aid options, but _technology does not replace good teaching methodologies_.

To that end, students will not always automatically know how to use technology for their own learning (Duffy, 2008). No matter how appealing or authentic a multimedia source is – be it a video, website, or other media – it will be minimally effective without careful pedagogical planning (Blake, 2008, p. 11). In other words, high-tech resources are like any teaching tool – they are only effective if you know how to use them properly within a carefully planned curriculum.

Before going any further, let’s review some helpful terminology, accompanied by relevant web links. You may run across some or all of this terminology at various times in your teaching career.

- **Computer-Assisted Language Learning (CALL):** Includes use of computer-based language programs such as ESL-specific software, educational podcasts, web pages, and CD-ROMs for educational purposes (Blake, 2008, p. 65).
- **Mobile-Assisted Language Learning (MALL):** Includes the use of mobile devices such as cellular mobile phones, tablets, etc. that are used for educational purposes (Ogata & Yano, 2005).
- **Computer-Mediated Communication (CMC):** Refers to using computer technology, such as web chat programs or web conferencing tools (e.g., Zoom, Skype), to communicate with others.
- **Podcasting:** Refers to downloading audio or video segments through an internet-based web syndication to view on the internet or on an MP3 player. Podcasts have a wide variety of uses such as assistance with pronunciation and targeted listening practices.
- **Video Chat:** Video chat programs such as Zoom, Skype, and Google Hangouts have been successfully integrated into many non-virtual ESL/EFL classrooms and have many uses (Eaton, 2010). Video chat can be used to let students view or listen to lectures from guest speakers from around the world, and most have chat features where students can type in questions as they speaker is lecturing. One high school in Japan has used Skype to communicate with American high school students and reports much success. Using this tool in a carefully prepared curriculum has reduced many students’ anxiety about conversing with native speakers of English, and ‘[the Japanese students] learned new vocabulary, English idioms, and how to manage conversations through authentic communication with American students’ (Tsukamoto, Nuspiiger, & Senzaki, 2009, p. 166). If your school has a subscription to another Webinar program, such as Elluminate, your guest speaker may have access to a virtual whiteboard and other helpful features.
- **Blogs:** Writings can be posted online in order to share personal experiences and opinions. They may also be considered as “… online hypertext journals that others read and react to” (Blake, 2008, p. 74).
- **Wikis:** Writings posted online can also allow multiple users to add content and edit the work. A wiki keeps a record of all modifications and allows a user to cancel out another person’s modification. Therefore, wikis facilitate collaborative writing and group projects.
