**What Research Says about Visual Aids**

Have you ever been in a lecture-based classroom where, after about 10 minutes of instruction, you found your mind wandering, and you had no idea what the instructor had said? What do you think would have helped to hold your interest or improve your understanding of the lecture material? Do you think that well-chosen visual aids would have helped in this situation, such as the use of maps, videos, colorful charts, or photos? Note that we say ‘well-chosen’ – there is an art to using both low-tech visual aids and technological tools. For instance, presenting PowerPoint slides filled with dense text is not an effective visual aid, as the audience cannot easily process dense text while listening to the speaker. We’ll discuss effective versus ineffective visuals later in this chapter.

How do visual aids enhance learning? First of all, well-chosen visual aids represent authentic language material, which is better than contrived material designed especially for the language classroom (Crawford, 2002). For example, if teaching a unit of conversational English around the topic of food, bringing in an authentic menu from a restaurant will be more interesting to students than the teacher inventing a menu or simply listing common restaurant-related vocabulary on the board. Beyond the authenticity of language that many visual aids can provide, many reputable studies support the use of visual aids in education. In simple terms, most students can acquire information using all senses, so only appealing to one’s sense of hearing can be limiting. In fact, the more senses that are activated during the learning process, the more likely the information will be stored in long-term memory (Canning-Wilson, 2001). Also, not all students are auditory learners, so simply hearing new material is not always an optimal approach. In fact, research has shown that visual aids included in presentations can increase retention from 14 to 38 percent (Silberman, 1996). Visual aids, in short, are an excellent way to heighten student interest, attention, motivation, and understanding.

In discussing visual aid use in the **ESL/EFL** classroom, we will start with “low-tech” (or “no-tech”) visual aids. Low-tech materials are defined as those that require little to no equipment to implement. They include word walls, pictures (from magazines or other sources), **realia**, flashcards, **graphic organizers**, cartoons, facial expressions and body language, and handwritten illustrations on flipcharts or boards. After that, we will explore common technological tools, which may be visual, auditory, or both. Technological tools require special equipment such as computers, internet, DVD players, overhead or computer-generated projectors, and audio players.
