**Low-Tech Visual Aids**

There are many kinds of low-tech visual aids suitable for the ESL/EFL classroom. This section will introduce you to the following common examples:

- **✔ Word Walls**

Visual aids are not necessarily expensive or complex. There are many low-cost options that just take some creativity and a few simple tools. First, if you have a classroom that you are allowed to decorate, you might set up word walls featuring key vocabulary terms with corresponding pictures for the current week or unit. The basic materials you need include construction paper and markers, as well as photos from magazines or the internet. Categories of word walls include high-frequency words, literature-based word walls, and content-area word walls. The wall can also be interactive so that as difficult words are encountered in a lesson, they can be added (Herrell & Jordan, 2004). Student projects can also be displayed in the classroom. **Conjugation** of common irregular verbs might be posted within easy sight, for example. The trick is to make sure your displays are not too cluttered – you want the displays to be visually appealing and easy to process. To learn more about ways you can integrate word walls into your classes, watch the following video.

- **✔ Magazine Cut-Outs**

An easy way to ensure a supply of visual aids for your teaching experience is to collect magazine photos and advertisements beforehand. To ensure they last, you can laminate them first. There are countless uses for such pictures. For example, if you are teaching grammatical structures of comparatives and superlatives, hand out photos that are comparable. Pictures of people and clothing are easy to compare. In reference to Figure 3, students might say, “the snow boots are the warmest shoes” or “I think flip flops are the most comfortable.” Students might either write their answers down or simply discuss them in small groups and then report their descriptions to the class.

Figure 3. Photos of shoes

Magazine advertisements can also be discussed or analyzed in small groups. For instance, during a unit on shopping or consumerism, you could put students in small conversation groups to figure out the target audience for the product (gender, age groups, income levels, etc.) and decide as a group whether the aid is effective or not. You could also have students compare two or three ads for the same type of product (acts, clothes, detergent, computers, restaurants, etc.) and determine which aid is better and what else **Figure 4** as examples for comparison or analysis. As a follow-up, students could design their own ad for the product using either personal photographs or images cut from magazines.

Figure 4. Drink advertisements

Magazine pictures can also inspire a creative writing activity. Ask students to make up a story based on a given picture or give them the beginning of a story and ask them to write the conclusion. Magazine pictures are also a good tool when requiring students to use certain new vocabulary words. For example, if students are learning about household items, give them a photo of a kitchen or living room displaying some of the target vocabulary, and ask them to describe what they see either in a descriptive paragraph or in a creative, fictional style.

Similar to magazine photos, personal photographs also spark a lot of discussion and interest. For example, a teacher could bring in photos from travel, and students could guess where they were taken, when the teacher traveled there, and what he or she did. Students’ personal photos can be used in the same type of activity. See the following sample activity:

**Guess the Picture**

Directions: Look at the picture. In small groups, guess the answers to the following questions. Then, we will read the responses together, including the true story, and you have to guess the real answers.

1. Who is this man?
2. Where is he?
3. What month and year is it?
4. What happened before this photo?
5. What is going to happen next?

- **✔ Realia**

*Realia* includes items from everyday life that students can view up close or handle during class. You can bring in **articles** of clothing, food containers, and small household gadgets – almost anything that is transportable and can be used to illustrate vocabulary or enhance a **role play**.

It can also be very effective to use personal realia, as the teacher and/or students can bring items for “show and tell”-type activities, which foster communication and build connections between teacher and students. - **✔ Flashcards**

Flashcards containing either pictures or words can also be valuable visual aids. The possibilities are numerous. For example, similar to writing a story based on a magazine picture, a student may be given an illustration of a story in three boxes, and he or she must write a few sentences to describe what is happening in the story. Imagine you are teaching a unit on safety in the workplace. You might have students make up a story using related vocabulary and pictures from flashcards (See Figure 5). In a different vocabulary exercise, students might be given cards with pictures of different professions on them. Working in pairs, each student picks a card, and they must guess their partner’s profession using 20 questions.

---

- **✔ Graphic Organizers**

Graphic organizers include tables, Venn diagrams, charts, timelines, and any graphic representation of a relationship between a set of ideas. In fact, graphic organizers might be given to students with some information already filled in. For instance, after reading a passage or listening to an audio clip, students are asked to fill in the missing information to illustrate their understanding. Graphic organizers, such as cluster diagrams, can be used to brainstorm ideas for writing assignment. There is a graphic organizer for virtually any purpose. Note that graphic organizers such as tables and charts require careful guidance and instruction by the instructor – don’t assume that all students understand how to read such visuals (Tang, 1991). - **✔ Cartoons**

Cartoons are excellent visuals for highlighting a topic at the beginning of a new unit or illustrating an idiom or play on words (as in Figure 6). You might even give students cartoons with the words missing and see if they can fill in the blanks to make a funny story. Can you think of other uses for cartoons in the classroom, such as political cartoons or daily cartoons from the newspaper?

---

- **✔ Facial Expressions and Body Language**

Even illustrating ideas with facial gestures or physical movement is considered a visual aid and has been proven to promote comprehension (Sueyoshi & Hardison, 2005). For instance, as students are learning about vocabulary for emotions, the teacher or students can imitate the emotion with facial expressions to show understanding. Similarly, any form of charades is an engaging visual. Imagine that your students are learning about expressions for household tasks. Students can take turns choosing a card that tells them to mimic a specific household chore, such as making the bed, setting the table, doing the dishes, vacuuming, doing the laundry, etc., while other students must guess the activity by describing it in a grammatically correct sentence, such as "Carlos is doing the dishes," or "Fatima is taking out the garbage."
