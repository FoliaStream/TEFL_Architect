**Video**

Next, let’s consider the educational benefits of using video from YouTube, podcasts, and other sources. Students respond well to video for many reasons, not least of which being the presence of non-verbal forms of communication, such as facial expressions, gestures, and posture (Crawford, 2002).

---

Videos also allow students to see the social context in which communication occurs, thus enhancing comprehension of spoken conversations (Crawford, 2002). Similarly, video can make topics more personal and accessible. For example, in one study, ESL students were given the opportunity to watch a video about an author in addition to reading about the author; this group was compared to another group of students who only read about the author. The students who saw the audio-visual resource tested better on comprehension of the content. The researchers attribute this partially to the “personalizing” effect of video; the author seemed more accessible to the students through video, which heightened the students’ interest in engaging with the author’s ideas. In this case, watching a video led to better understanding of the material (Inglese, Mayer, & Rigotti, 2007).

There are guidelines to follow for using video, whether you are showing a news clip, a TV show, or longer film. Perhaps the most obvious guideline is to be sure the video you choose is of adequate quality and aligns with the objectives of the lesson (Duffy, 2008). For instance, if you have been teaching a unit focusing on the vocabulary of food and restaurants, you could show \( x^2 = 0.4 \) minute segment from a cooking show, a restaurant review, or a food-focused movie. You’ll want to break up that clip into shorter segments (often 15-30 seconds each) and pause to discuss new vocabulary and click comprehension. More advanced students will be able to process longer segments (King, 2003).

To generate interest and trigger schematia, consider asking students to guess what might happen based on the title of the clip or use a freeze frame and have them guess what they will watch. Teachers should also provide guidance with new vocabulary, idioms, and grammar as needed to ensure understanding of the content prior to viewing. To enhance vocabulary acquisition, you may also want to precede the video with a reading activity in which students are exposed to the main vocabulary they will encounter in the film (Li, 2009).

During viewing, make sure that students have an **objective** in mind, whether it is to take brief notes on the plot, to answer true/false comprehension questions, or to prepare to discuss the motivation behind the character actions. Follow up each film segment with a brief small group or whole-class discussion of some sort, such as checking for comprehension, predicting what will come next, or guessing the motivation of certain characters.

There are many ways you can liven up video viewing for your students. For example, you can play a video with the audio off and narrate the video, especially if it shows steps in a process. In addition, with the audio off, you can ask students to guess what the speaker was saying. Another idea is to play the audio track first with the video hidden; students then guess what the images will be and compare guesses before watching the video (Duffy, 2008).

To promote independent learning, you might direct more advanced students to find a YouTube video on a specific topic being studied and ask them to compile a list of difficult vocabulary words before presenting the video to the rest of the group during a future class (Duffy, 2008, pp. 39-40).

There is some debate around the use of English captioning during film viewing. King (2002) points out that turning on English captions while viewing scenes will help students learn new vocabulary and idioms, as well as develop word recognition. On the flip side, the use of captioning changes a listening activity into a reading activity where the captioning becomes a “crucial.” Without relying on the “crucial” of English captioning, students will gain confidence in their listening abilities and the activity will “promote active viewing and listening for keywords and main ideas” (King, 2002). Finally, consider the middle ground in this debate. An instructor might use captioning for only select scenes or only after having students guess the dialogue without the help of captioning.
